# Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** states that certain pairs of physical properties — most famously position $x$ and momentum $p$ — cannot both be known to arbitrary precision simultaneously. Quantitatively,

$$
\Delta x \, \Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

where $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ represent the uncertainty (standard deviation, in a statistical sense) in position and momentum respectively, and $\hbar = h/2\pi$ is the reduced Planck constant.

## What this actually means

This is not a statement about measurement technique or instrument imperfection — even with a hypothetically perfect measuring device, a quantum state that is very sharply localized in position is mathematically guaranteed to have a very spread-out distribution of momentum, and vice versa. It's a direct mathematical consequence of describing particles with wavefunctions, related to the same trade-off that shows up for classical waves (a wave pulse localized in space necessarily contains a broad spread of wavelengths — this is a general property of Fourier analysis, not something unique to quantum mechanics). Quantum mechanics attaches this trade-off to a physical, measurable limit rather than a mathematical curiosity of describing something as a wave packet.

## Why it matters

The uncertainty principle rules out the classical idea of a particle following a precise trajectory with simultaneously well-defined position and momentum at every instant — a genuinely different picture of motion than anything in [[Kinematics Basics|classical kinematics]]. It also underlies phenomena like the finite, non-zero minimum energy of a confined quantum system (see [[Quantum Harmonic Oscillator]]) — a particle confined to a small region can't have exactly zero momentum, or its position uncertainty would need to be infinite, so confinement always implies some minimum "zero-point" energy.

Similar uncertainty relations exist for other pairs of observables, notably energy and time.
