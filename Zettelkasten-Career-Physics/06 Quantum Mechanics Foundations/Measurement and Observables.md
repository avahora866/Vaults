# Measurement and Observables

Quantum mechanics makes a sharp distinction, absent in classical physics, between a system's state (its full mathematical description, potentially a superposition) and what happens when you **measure** it.

## Observables and probabilities

A measurable physical quantity — position, momentum, energy, spin — is called an **observable**, represented mathematically by an operator acting on the quantum state. When you measure an observable, the possible outcomes are the operator's **eigenvalues** (fixed, discrete or continuous allowed values), and the state "collapses" onto the corresponding **eigenstate** — the specific state associated with the outcome you got. Crucially, quantum mechanics generally can't predict *which* outcome you'll get on any single measurement, only the **probability** of each possible outcome, computed from the state's wavefunction (specifically, the squared magnitude of its overlap with each possible eigenstate).

This is the **measurement postulate**: measurement is not a passive readout of a pre-existing value (as in classical physics) — it actively, probabilistically selects one outcome from the superposition and forces the system into the corresponding state.

## Why this is genuinely strange

Repeating the identical measurement on identically prepared systems can yield different results each time, with only the *statistics* over many repetitions being predictable — a stark departure from classical determinism, where identical starting conditions always produce identical outcomes. Interpreting exactly *what happens* during measurement (the "measurement problem") remains one of the deepest open conceptual questions in physics, though the *predictions* quantum mechanics makes about measurement statistics are extremely well tested and not in doubt.

## The uncertainty principle

Some pairs of observables (position and momentum, most famously) can't both be known precisely at the same time — not due to any measurement imperfection, but as a fundamental mathematical feature of quantum states. See [[Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle]].
