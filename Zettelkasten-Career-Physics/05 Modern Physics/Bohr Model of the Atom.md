# Bohr Model of the Atom

Classically, an electron orbiting a nucleus (like a planet orbiting the sun, held by an attractive inverse-square force) should continuously radiate electromagnetic energy and spiral into the nucleus within a fraction of a second — atoms, by this logic, shouldn't be stable. They obviously are, so something was missing.

Niels Bohr proposed (1913) that electrons can only occupy certain specific, discrete orbits — each with a fixed, quantized energy — and that an electron in one of these allowed orbits simply doesn't radiate, no matter how classical electromagnetism might expect it to. For hydrogen, the allowed energy levels are

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

where $n$ is a positive integer (the **principal quantum number**) labelling each allowed orbit/energy level.

## Explaining atomic spectra

An electron can jump between these fixed levels only by absorbing or emitting a photon whose energy exactly equals the energy difference between the initial and final levels:

$$
hf = E_{n_i} - E_{n_f}
$$

Because only specific energy differences are possible, atoms absorb and emit light only at specific, discrete frequencies — the **spectral lines** long observed in atomic spectra but previously unexplained. This was the first quantitative success of the emerging quantum picture, correctly predicting hydrogen's spectral lines to high precision.

## Limits of the model

The Bohr model is a useful conceptual bridge, but it's not the full story — it doesn't correctly predict spectra for atoms with more than one electron, and its picture of electrons in fixed circular orbits is superseded by the [[Schrodinger Equation and State Evolution|Schrödinger equation]], which instead describes electrons via probability clouds (orbitals) rather than sharp orbits. Bohr's key insight — that atomic energy is quantized into discrete levels — survives fully intact in the modern theory.
