# Energy Quantization

Classically, energy is assumed to be continuous — a system can have any amount of energy, adjustable by arbitrarily small increments. **Energy quantization** is the discovery that, at the atomic and subatomic scale, energy instead comes in discrete, indivisible packets for certain systems and interactions.

## Planck's hypothesis

The first crack appeared in **blackbody radiation** — the spectrum of light emitted by any hot object. Classical physics predicted this spectrum should diverge at high frequencies (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"), which plainly doesn't happen. Max Planck found the correct spectrum could be derived only by assuming that the energy exchanged between radiation and matter comes in discrete quanta, each of energy $E = hf$, proportional to frequency. See [[Plancks Hypothesis]].

## Atomic energy levels: the Bohr model

Applying a similar quantization idea to atoms, Niels Bohr proposed that electrons in an atom can only occupy specific, discrete orbits — each with a definite, fixed energy — rather than any arbitrary orbit. Electrons jump between these allowed energy levels by absorbing or emitting a photon whose energy exactly matches the energy difference between levels, explaining why atoms absorb and emit light only at specific, characteristic frequencies (**spectral lines**) rather than a continuous range. See [[Bohr Model of the Atom]].

## Why quantization matters

Energy quantization is the phenomenon that gives quantum mechanics its name. It's directly responsible for the stability of atoms (electrons can't spiral continuously into the nucleus, because there's a lowest allowed energy level), the discrete spectral lines used to identify chemical elements (including in distant stars), and — closer to engineering applications — the discrete, addressable energy levels ("0" and "1") that physical qubits are built from.
