# Modern Physics

By the end of the 19th century, classical mechanics and electromagnetism seemed to explain almost everything. **Modern physics** is the collection of discoveries, starting around 1900, where that classical picture broke down — where light behaved like particles, matter behaved like waves, energy came in discrete chunks rather than a continuum, and space and time turned out not to be absolute. These discoveries motivated, and were eventually unified by, quantum mechanics.

## Wave-particle duality

Light, long understood as a wave (see [[Electromagnetic Waves]]), was shown to also behave like a stream of discrete particles (**photons**) in certain experiments — most famously the photoelectric effect. Conversely, matter — electrons, atoms, even large molecules — was shown to exhibit wave-like behaviour (interference, diffraction) under the right conditions. Neither the "pure wave" nor the "pure particle" picture is fully correct; both are limiting descriptions of a single underlying quantum reality. See [[Wave-Particle Duality]].

## Energy quantization

Max Planck found that treating the energy exchanged between light and matter as coming in discrete packets — quanta — rather than a continuous flow, resolved a long-standing puzzle in the physics of hot glowing objects (blackbody radiation). This quantization idea, extended by Bohr to explain why atoms only emit and absorb light at specific, discrete frequencies, was the crack that eventually opened into full quantum theory. See [[Energy Quantization]].

## Special relativity

Einstein showed that the laws of physics, and in particular the speed of light, are the same for all observers moving at constant velocity relative to each other — a requirement that forces space and time to no longer be absolute, but to depend on the observer's motion. Time runs slower, and lengths contract, for objects moving relative to you. See [[Special Relativity]].

## Why "modern physics" is a bridge

These topics sit at the boundary between the classical world (Mechanics, Waves, Electromagnetism, Thermodynamics) and the full quantum theory built up in [[Quantum Mechanics Foundations]]. Energy quantization directly motivates the Schrödinger equation's discrete energy solutions; wave-particle duality directly motivates describing quantum states with a wavefunction obeying wave-like superposition. Special relativity, while not itself part of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, becomes essential once quantum theory is extended to high-energy or high-precision regimes (quantum field theory, GPS satellite timing corrections, etc.).
