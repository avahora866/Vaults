# De Broglie Matter Waves

If light — usually understood as a wave — could behave like particles (as the photoelectric effect showed), Louis de Broglie asked whether the reverse might also hold: could particles of matter, like electrons, behave like waves? He proposed that any particle with momentum $p$ has an associated wavelength:

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p}
$$

where $h$ is Planck's constant. This **de Broglie wavelength** is the same formula that relates a photon's momentum to its wavelength, now applied to matter generally.

## Why we don't notice it in everyday life

For everyday objects, $\lambda$ is absurdly small — a thrown baseball has a de Broglie wavelength many orders of magnitude smaller than an atomic nucleus, far too small to produce any observable wave effect. For an electron, though, $\lambda$ can be comparable to atomic dimensions, which is why wave effects (interference, diffraction) are readily observable for electrons but never for baseballs.

## Experimental confirmation

This was confirmed by the Davisson-Germer experiment, which fired electrons at a crystal and observed a diffraction pattern — a signature wave phenomenon — exactly as predicted by treating the electrons as waves with de Broglie's wavelength. Electron microscopes exploit this directly: because electrons can have much shorter wavelengths than visible light (at practical energies), they can resolve far finer detail than any optical microscope.

This idea — that matter has an associated wave — is the direct conceptual seed for the **wavefunction** in quantum mechanics (see [[Quantum States]]), which generalises "matter wave" into a full probabilistic description of a quantum system's state.
