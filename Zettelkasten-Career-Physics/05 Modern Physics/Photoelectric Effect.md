# Photoelectric Effect

When light shines on certain metal surfaces, electrons are ejected — the **photoelectric effect**. Classically (treating light purely as a wave), you'd expect brighter light (carrying more energy overall) to eventually eject electrons at any frequency, given enough time to accumulate energy. Experimentally, this isn't what happens:

* Below a certain **threshold frequency** $f_0$ (which depends on the metal), *no* electrons are ejected, no matter how intense the light is.
* Above the threshold, electrons are ejected instantly, and increasing the light's intensity increases the *number* of ejected electrons, not their individual energy.
* The ejected electrons' maximum kinetic energy increases linearly with the light's *frequency*, not its intensity.

## Einstein's explanation

Einstein explained this (building on Planck's idea of energy quanta) by proposing that light itself consists of discrete packets — **photons** — each carrying energy

$$
E = hf
$$

where $h$ is Planck's constant. A single photon either has enough energy to knock an electron free (if $hf$ exceeds the metal's **work function** $\phi$, the minimum energy binding an electron) or it doesn't — intensity (more photons per second) doesn't help a single photon deliver more energy than $hf$. The ejected electron's maximum kinetic energy is

$$
KE_{max} = hf - \phi
$$

This was one of the first clear pieces of evidence that light carries energy in discrete quanta rather than continuously, and it directly motivated the particle side of [[Wave-Particle Duality]].
