# Special Relativity

Classical (Newtonian) mechanics assumes space and time are absolute — a universal clock ticks the same for everyone, and distances are the same for everyone, regardless of how they're moving. **Special relativity**, developed by Einstein in 1905, showed this isn't true.

## The two postulates

Special relativity rests on two assumptions:

1. The laws of physics are the same in all **inertial reference frames** (frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other) — no experiment performed inside a smoothly moving frame can tell you how fast (or whether) it's moving relative to anything else.
2. The speed of light in vacuum, $c$, is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the light source or the observer.

The second postulate is the radical one — classically, if you run toward a wave source, you'd expect to measure a higher wave speed relative to yourself. Light doesn't behave this way: everyone measures the same $c$, no matter how they're moving.

## Consequences: time dilation and length contraction

Making the speed of light the same for all observers forces space and time themselves to become observer-dependent. A clock moving relative to you appears to run slow (**time dilation**), and objects moving relative to you appear contracted along their direction of motion (**length contraction**). See [[Time Dilation and Length Contraction]] for the details.

## Mass-energy equivalence

Special relativity also produces perhaps the most famous equation in physics,

$$
E = mc^2
$$

showing that mass itself is a form of energy — an enormously concentrated one, given how large $c^2$ is. This underlies nuclear reactions (fission and fusion release energy by converting a tiny amount of mass into a large amount of energy) and sets an absolute energy cost to accelerating any massive object toward the speed of light.

## Why this is worth knowing, even briefly

Special relativity isn't part of non-relativistic quantum mechanics (the version covered in [[02 Quantum Mechanics Foundations]]), but it becomes unavoidable at high particle energies (particle physics, quantum field theory) and even shows up in everyday technology — GPS satellites must correct for both special- and general-relativistic time dilation to stay accurate.
