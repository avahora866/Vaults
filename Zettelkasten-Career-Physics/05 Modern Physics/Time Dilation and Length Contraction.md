# Time Dilation and Length Contraction

Two of the most striking consequences of special relativity's postulates (see [[Special Relativity]]) are that time and space themselves depend on the observer's relative motion.

## Time dilation

A clock moving at speed $v$ relative to an observer appears, to that observer, to run slow. If $\Delta t_0$ is the time interval measured in the clock's own rest frame (**proper time**), the interval measured by an observer watching it move is

$$
\Delta t = \gamma \Delta t_0, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}
$$

The **Lorentz factor** $\gamma$ is always $\geq 1$ and grows dramatically as $v$ approaches $c$, but is essentially indistinguishable from 1 at everyday speeds — which is why time dilation is never noticed in ordinary life, only in high-precision measurements (atomic clocks on fast-moving aircraft, particle physics) or at speeds close to $c$.

## Length contraction

An object moving at speed $v$ relative to an observer appears contracted along its direction of motion (though not perpendicular to it):

$$
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}
$$

where $L_0$ is the object's **proper length** (measured in its own rest frame).

## Important subtlety: it's relative

Both effects are genuinely symmetric between observers — from the moving clock's own perspective, it's the *other* observer's clock that appears to run slow, not its own. There's no universal, "correct" frame from which to judge whose clock is "really" running slow; it depends entirely on which reference frame you're measuring from. (Resolving apparent paradoxes arising from this symmetry, like the twin paradox, requires careful attention to acceleration and simultaneity — a fuller treatment than this note attempts.)

## Practical relevance

GPS satellites experience measurable time dilation (moving fast relative to the ground) that must be corrected for continuously to keep positioning accurate — without the correction, GPS position errors would accumulate by kilometres per day.
