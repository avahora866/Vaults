# Resonance

**Resonance** occurs when a system is driven by a periodic force whose frequency matches (or nearly matches) the system's own **natural frequency** of oscillation — the frequency it would oscillate at on its own, undriven. At resonance, even a small driving force can build up to a very large amplitude of oscillation, because each push arrives in step with the existing motion and reinforces it.

For a damped, driven oscillator, the response amplitude as a function of driving frequency $\omega_{drive}$ peaks sharply near the natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$; how sharp and how tall that peak is depends on the amount of damping — lightly damped systems show a tall, narrow resonance peak, heavily damped systems a broad, shallow one.

**Everyday and engineering examples**: pushing a swing at just the right moments, a wine glass shattering at its resonant frequency, radio tuning circuits selecting one station's frequency out of many, and — famously — the 1940 Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse, driven by wind-induced oscillations near the bridge's natural frequency. In engineering and robotics, avoiding unwanted resonance (or deliberately exploiting it) is a standard design consideration wherever moving or vibrating parts are involved.

Resonance is also central to understanding **energy quantization** in quantum systems: an atom absorbs a photon most efficiently when the photon's frequency matches the energy gap between two atomic states — a direct, if reinterpreted, echo of classical resonance.
