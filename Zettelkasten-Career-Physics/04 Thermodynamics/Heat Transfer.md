# Heat Transfer

Heat — energy transferred due to a temperature difference — moves between objects or regions by three distinct mechanisms.

## Conduction

**Conduction** transfers heat through direct contact, as faster-moving (hotter) particles collide with and transfer energy to slower-moving (cooler) neighbours, without any bulk motion of the material itself. The rate of conductive heat flow through a material depends on its **thermal conductivity**, cross-sectional area, temperature difference, and thickness:

$$
\frac{Q}{t} = kA\frac{\Delta T}{d}
$$

Metals conduct heat well (free electrons carry energy efficiently); insulators like wood or foam conduct poorly, which is precisely why they're used for insulation.

## Convection

**Convection** transfers heat via bulk movement of a fluid (liquid or gas). Warm fluid, typically less dense, rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it, setting up a circulating flow that carries thermal energy along with the moving material itself. This drives weather patterns, ocean currents, and the mixing in a pot of boiling water, and is the main heat-loss mechanism engineers account for when designing cooling systems with fans or fluid loops.

## Radiation

**Radiation** transfers heat via electromagnetic waves (see [[Electromagnetic Waves]]) and, uniquely among the three mechanisms, requires no medium at all — it works through a vacuum. Every object above absolute zero radiates electromagnetic energy, with the power radiated scaling with the fourth power of absolute temperature (the Stefan-Boltzmann law). This is how the Sun heats the Earth across empty space, and why objects glow visibly when hot enough (their thermal radiation shifts into the visible spectrum).
