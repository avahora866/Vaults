# Entropy and Irreversibility

**Entropy** ($S$) is often introduced thermodynamically as a quantity that increases when heat flows into a system at temperature $T$: $dS = \delta Q_{rev}/T$ for a reversible process. But its deeper, more intuitive meaning comes from statistical mechanics: entropy measures the number of microscopic arrangements (**microstates**) consistent with a system's observed macroscopic state:

$$
S = k_B \ln \Omega
$$

where $\Omega$ is the number of microstates and $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant. A tidy, highly ordered macroscopic state (like all gas molecules confined to one corner of a room) corresponds to relatively few possible microscopic arrangements — low entropy. A disordered state (gas spread evenly through the room) corresponds to an astronomically larger number of possible arrangements — high entropy.

## Why processes are irreversible

The **second law of thermodynamics** says entropy never decreases in an isolated system. This isn't a separate mechanical law forbidding certain motions — it's a statistical statement: starting from an ordered (low-entropy) state, there are simply vastly more disordered configurations than ordered ones for the system to evolve into, so it overwhelmingly tends toward higher entropy, even though the underlying particle collisions are individually reversible. Gas spontaneously spreads to fill a room; it never spontaneously gathers itself into a corner — not because the physics of individual collisions forbids it, but because that outcome is staggeringly improbable among all the ways the particles could be arranged.

This is the physical origin of the "arrow of time" in everyday experience — the fact that we remember the past but not the future, and that broken glass doesn't spontaneously reassemble.
