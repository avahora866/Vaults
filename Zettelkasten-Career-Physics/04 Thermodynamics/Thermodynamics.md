# Thermodynamics

**Thermodynamics** studies energy, heat, and work in systems, especially how heat flows and how energy transforms between useful (mechanical, electrical) and less useful (thermal) forms. It was developed to understand steam engines and remains foundational to essentially all engineering that involves energy conversion — engines, refrigerators, power plants, electronics cooling.

## The laws of thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is built on a small number of remarkably general laws:

* **First law**: energy is conserved — the change in a system's internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system, $\Delta U = Q - W$. This is just conservation of energy applied to heat and work explicitly.
* **Second law**: heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold, never the reverse (without external work), and the **entropy** of an isolated system never decreases. This law introduces an arrow of time into physics — a directionality most of the rest of physics's equations don't have.

See [[Laws of Thermodynamics]] and [[Entropy and Irreversibility]].

## Heat transfer

Heat moves between objects or regions by three mechanisms: **conduction** (direct molecular contact, e.g. through a solid), **convection** (bulk movement of a fluid carrying heat with it), and **radiation** (electromagnetic waves, requiring no medium at all — it's how the Sun heats the Earth through the vacuum of space). See [[Heat Transfer]].

## Statistical mechanics

Thermodynamics describes bulk properties like temperature, pressure, and entropy — but these emerge from the statistical behaviour of enormous numbers of individual particles. **Statistical mechanics** provides that microscopic foundation, explaining temperature as related to the average kinetic energy of particles, and entropy as related to the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with a given macroscopic state. See [[Statistical Mechanics]].

## Why this matters broadly

Thermodynamics underlies engine design, refrigeration, materials science, and chemical engineering. Its statistical foundation is also directly relevant to quantum mechanics: many advanced quantum topics (thermal states, decoherence, the behaviour of quantum systems coupled to a noisy environment) are essentially statistical mechanics applied to quantum states.
