# Magnetic Fields and Moving Charges

Unlike electric fields, which act on any charge, **magnetic fields** only exert a force on charges that are *moving* (or on other magnetic sources). A magnetic field $\vec{B}$ is produced by moving charge (current) and, in permanent magnets, by the aligned intrinsic magnetic moments (spins) of electrons within the material.

## The magnetic force on a moving charge

A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ through a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ feels a force

$$
\vec{F} = q\vec{v} \times \vec{B}
$$

Because this is a cross product, the force is always perpendicular to both the velocity and the field — a magnetic field can change a charge's *direction* of motion but never its speed, since the force never has a component along the direction of travel. This is why charged particles move in circles (or helices) in a uniform magnetic field, rather than speeding up or slowing down.

## Currents and fields

A current-carrying wire creates a magnetic field circling around it (Ampère's law describes this quantitatively), and conversely a current-carrying wire in an external magnetic field experiences a force — this is the operating principle of **electric motors**: current through a wire loop in a magnetic field produces a torque that spins the loop.

Running the idea in reverse — moving a wire (or changing a magnetic field) to *induce* a current — is **electromagnetic induction**, the operating principle of **generators** and transformers, and the deeper reason electric and magnetic fields can't really be treated as fully separate phenomena; they're two aspects of a single electromagnetic field, related by how fast each changes over time. This interplay is exactly what allows [[Electromagnetic Waves|electromagnetic waves]] to exist and propagate through empty space.
