# Energy in Electric and Magnetic Fields

Electric and magnetic fields are not just bookkeeping devices for computing forces — they genuinely store energy, recoverable as work. This is most concretely seen in two everyday circuit components: capacitors and inductors.

## Energy in a capacitor

A **capacitor** stores energy in the electric field between two oppositely charged conductive plates. For a capacitor of capacitance $C$ holding charge $Q$ at voltage $V$:

$$
U_C = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C}
$$

This energy comes from the work done separating charge onto the plates against their mutual attraction — see [[Electric Potential Energy]].

## Energy in an inductor

An **inductor** (a coil of wire) stores energy in the magnetic field generated by current flowing through it:

$$
U_L = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2
$$

where $L$ is the inductance and $I$ the current. This energy comes from the work done establishing the current against the coil's own opposition to a *change* in current (self-inductance).

See [[Capacitors and Inductors]] for how these two components behave in circuits, and why they matter for both classical electronics and quantum hardware.

## Why this generalises

More generally, any electric or magnetic field stores energy proportional to the square of the field strength, spread continuously through the space the field occupies — not localized at the charges or currents that created it. This "energy lives in the field" perspective is one of the conceptual bridges from classical electromagnetism into field theory more broadly, including the quantum treatment of the electromagnetic field.
