**3D Cartesian coordinates** are a system for defining the position of a point in three-dimensional space. They are based on three mutually [[perpendicular]] axes (usually labeled $x$, $y$, and $z$).

### **Coordinate Axes**

* **x-axis**: Horizontal axis (left-right direction)
* **y-axis**: Vertical axis (up-down direction)
* **z-axis**: Depth axis (forward-backward direction)

The axes are perpendicular to each other and intersect at the **origin** $(0, 0, 0)$.

### **Point in Space**

A point $P$ in 3D space can be represented as:

$$
P(x, y, z)
$$

Where:

* $x$ is the position along the **x-axis**
* $y$ is the position along the **y-axis**
* $z$ is the position along the **z-axis**

### **How It Works**

To describe any point in 3D space:

* The point $P(x, y, z)$ means that it is located at:

  * **x** units along the **x-axis**
  * **y** units along the **y-axis**
  * **z** units along the **z-axis**

---

### **Visualizing 3D Coordinates**

* Imagine a **cube** where each corner represents a point in 3D space.
* The three axes define a "grid" that organizes space. Each point's position is determined by how far along each axis the point lies.

### **Example**

* **Point (2, 3, 4)**:

  * Move 2 units along the x-axis
  * Move 3 units along the y-axis
  * Move 4 units along the z-axis

This describes a unique location in 3D space.

---

### **Applications in Physics and Quantum Mechanics**

* In **quantum mechanics**, the position of a particle in space can be represented using 3D coordinates.
* A particle's **wavefunction** might depend on the 3D coordinates, $\psi(x, y, z)$, which gives the probability of finding the particle at a particular point in space.

### **Distance Between Two Points in 3D**

The distance between two points $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ in 3D space is given by:

$$
d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}
$$

This formula is derived from the Pythagorean theorem extended to 3D.

---

### **Key Takeaways**

* **3D Cartesian coordinates** use three perpendicular axes to define positions in space: $x$, $y$, and $z$.
* They are crucial in **classical mechanics**, **quantum mechanics**, and **geometry** to describe points, vectors, and movement in space.
