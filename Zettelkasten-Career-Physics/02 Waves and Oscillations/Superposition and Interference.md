# Superposition and Interference

The **principle of superposition** states that when two or more waves overlap in the same region, the net displacement at any point is simply the sum of the displacements each wave would produce individually. This holds for any *linear* wave system — waves whose amplitude isn't too large to distort the medium's own response.

## Constructive and destructive interference

When two waves arrive in phase (crest aligning with crest), their amplitudes add — **constructive interference**, producing a larger combined wave. When they arrive exactly out of phase (crest aligning with trough), they cancel — **destructive interference**. Between these extremes lies a continuum of partial reinforcement or cancellation, depending on the phase difference between the waves.

For two waves of the same frequency and amplitude $A$, differing in phase by $\delta$, the resulting amplitude is

$$
A_{net} = 2A\left|\cos\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right|
$$

which is maximal ($2A$) when $\delta = 0$ and zero when $\delta = \pi$.

## Where this shows up

Interference is the basis of the famous **double-slit experiment**: light passing through two closely spaced slits produces alternating bright and dark fringes on a screen, direct evidence of light's wave nature (and, when performed with single particles, direct evidence of quantum superposition — see [[Wave-Particle Duality]]). It's also the principle behind noise-cancelling headphones (deliberately generating destructive interference against unwanted sound) and anti-reflective lens coatings.
