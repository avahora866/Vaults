# Waves and Oscillations

A **wave** is a disturbance that propagates through space and time, carrying energy without necessarily carrying matter along with it. Where [[Simple Harmonic Motion|SHM]] describes a single oscillator, a wave can be thought of as that same oscillatory pattern spread continuously across many connected points — a rope, a body of water, air, or empty space (for light).

## Describing a wave

A wave is characterised by a few key quantities:

* **Wavelength** ($\lambda$): the distance between successive identical points on the wave (e.g. crest to crest).
* **Frequency** ($f$): how many complete cycles pass a fixed point per second, measured in hertz.
* **Amplitude**: the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
* **Speed** ($v$): how fast the wave pattern itself travels, related to wavelength and frequency by

$$
v = f\lambda
$$

For a sinusoidal wave travelling in the $+x$ direction, the displacement is often written

$$
y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \quad \omega = 2\pi f
$$

## Transverse vs. longitudinal waves

In a **transverse wave**, the medium oscillates perpendicular to the direction the wave travels (e.g. a wave on a string, or light — an electromagnetic wave). In a **longitudinal wave**, the medium oscillates parallel to the direction of travel — the classic example is sound, which propagates as alternating compressions and rarefactions of air.

## Superposition

When two or more waves overlap, the resulting displacement is simply the sum of the individual displacements — the **principle of superposition**. This is what allows waves to interfere constructively (amplifying each other) or destructively (cancelling each other), and it's the mathematical basis for [[Standing Waves|standing waves]]. See [[Superposition and Interference]].

## The Doppler effect

When a wave source and observer are in relative motion, the observed frequency shifts — higher when they approach, lower when they recede. See [[Doppler Effect]].

## Why waves matter broadly

Wave behaviour is one of the most universal patterns in physics: it governs sound, light, water, seismic waves, and — expressed through the Schrödinger equation — the probability amplitudes of quantum mechanics itself. The formalism built here (superposition, interference, standing waves) reappears almost unchanged in [[Quantum Mechanics Foundations|quantum mechanics]], just applied to a wavefunction instead of a physical displacement.
