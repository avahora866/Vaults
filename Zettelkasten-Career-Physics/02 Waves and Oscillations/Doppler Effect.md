# Doppler Effect

The **Doppler effect** is the shift in observed frequency of a wave when its source and observer are in relative motion. A source approaching an observer compresses the wave crests together in front of it, raising the observed frequency (pitch, for sound); a receding source stretches them out, lowering it.

For sound, with source speed $v_s$, observer speed $v_o$, and wave speed $v$ in the medium:

$$
f_{observed} = f_{source}\left(\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}\right)
$$

with signs chosen depending on whether source and observer are approaching or receding.

**Everyday example**: the change in pitch of a passing ambulance siren — higher as it approaches, lower as it recedes.

**Broader applications**: the Doppler effect is used in radar speed guns, medical Doppler ultrasound (measuring blood flow speed), and — for light rather than sound — the **redshift** and **blueshift** of light from receding or approaching astronomical objects, which is how astronomers measure the expansion of the universe. The same underlying physics applies to any wave phenomenon, with light requiring a relativistic version of the formula since there's no medium for light to travel "through."
