AHP works by structuring decision problems into a hierarchy, allowing for a logical breakdown of the problem. Pairwise comparisons enable decision-makers to articulate subjective judgments systematically, which are then translated into numerical values. These values are used to prioritize criteria and alternatives.

For example, when choosing a new car, the goal ("Select the best car") might be broken down into criteria like **price**, **fuel efficiency**, and **comfort**. Alternatives (specific car models) are compared for each criterion, and the process reveals the most suitable option based on the weighted criteria.

# Key Steps in AHP
1. **Define the Goal**: Clearly state the main objective or decision to be made.
2. **Identify Criteria and Alternatives**: List the criteria that influence the decision and the available options (alternatives).
3. **Create a Hierarchy**: Organize the goal at the top level, criteria at the middle level, and alternatives at the bottom.
4. **Perform Pairwise Comparisons**:
    - Compare each criterion against every other criterion to assess their relative importance.
    - Use a scale (e.g., 1 to 9) where 1 indicates equal importance, and 9 indicates extreme preference.
    - Similarly, compare alternatives for each criterion.
5. **Calculate Weights**:
    - Normalize the pairwise comparison matrix to derive priority weights for each criterion.
    - Combine the weights of criteria with the scores of alternatives to rank the options.
6. **Consistency Check**: Verify the consistency of judgments in the pairwise comparisons. If inconsistencies are high, revise the inputs.
7. **Make a Decision**: Select the alternative with the highest overall priority score.

# Strengths of AHP
- Handles both qualitative and quantitative factors.
- Provides a systematic approach to complex decisions.
- Allows for group decision-making and consensus.

# Limitations
- Relies on subjective judgments, which may introduce bias.
- Requires consistency in pairwise comparisons, which can be challenging.
