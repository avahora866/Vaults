Cognitive dissonance is the psychological tension or mental discomfort you experience when you hold two or more conflicting beliefs, values, or ideas simultaneously. It also occurs when your behaviour contradicts your beliefs.

[[The Human Mind and Consistency|Because the human mind strives for internal consistency]], this conflict triggers a drive to resolve the discomfort. You don't just "prefer" consistency; your brain actively demands it to reduce the stress of the mismatch.
# The Origin: Festinger (1957)
Psychologist Leon Festinger introduced the theory in 1957. His most famous demonstration was the **Festinger–Carlsmith experiment (1959)**:
* Participants performed an excruciatingly boring task (turning pegs) for an hour.
* They were paid either **$1** or **$20** to lie to the next participant and say the task was fun.
* **Result:** Those paid $20 admitted the task was boring (money justified the lie). Those paid $1 actually convinced themselves the task *was* fun.
* **Why?** $1 wasn't enough justification to lie, so they experienced dissonance. To resolve it, they changed their internal belief about the task.

# How You Reduce Dissonance
When you feel this tension, you typically use one of three methods to fix it.

| Strategy | Mechanism | Example: You are a smoker but know it causes cancer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Change Behavior** | You align your actions with your beliefs. | You quit smoking immediately. |
| **Change Belief** | You discard the conflicting belief. | You convince yourself that the medical research is flawed or "fake news." |
| **Add New Cognition** | You rationalize the behavior by adding a third idea that bridges the gap. | "I eat well and exercise, so that cancels out the smoking risk," or "I'd rather live a short, happy life than a long, miserable one." |
# Common Real-World Scenarios
* **"Sour Grapes" (Adaptive Preference):** Derived from Aesop's Fable. A fox cannot reach grapes he wants, so he decides they were probably sour anyway. You desire something you can't have, so you criticize it to reduce the pain of rejection.
* **Buyer's Remorse (Post-Decision Dissonance):** After making a difficult purchase (like a car or stock investment), you may obsessively read positive reviews or ignore negative news about the product to justify your decision and silence the doubt.
* **Hazing / Effort Justification:** If you work incredibly hard or suffer to join a group (fraternity, military, elite club), you will value membership more highly to justify the pain endured.

# Criticism and Nuance
While widely accepted, the theory has limitations:
* **Measurement Difficulty:** It is difficult to objectively measure the level of psychological "discomfort" a person feels.
* **Self-Perception Theory (Bem):** Critics argue that people don't always feel "tension." Instead, they simply observe their own behavior to infer their attitudes (e.g., "I ate the whole sandwich, so I must have been hungry," rather than "I ate it to resolve stress").
* **Cultural Variance:** Individualistic cultures (like the US/UK) tend to feel more dissonance regarding personal choices than collectivist cultures, where inconsistency might be tolerated for the sake of group harmony.
