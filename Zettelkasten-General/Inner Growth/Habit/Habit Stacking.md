**Habit stacking** is a method for building new habits by attaching them to existing ones. The idea is that instead of creating a routine from scratch, you *stack* a new desired behavior onto a habit you already do consistently.

This leverages your existing neural pathways, making the new behavior easier to remember and more likely to stick. The technique comes from behavioral science and has been popularized by authors like BJ Fogg and James Clear.

---

# Core Concept

> **"After \[current habit], I will \[new habit]."**

This structure uses a clear **cue** (the existing habit) to trigger a new action. This takes advantage of how habits naturally work: **cue → routine → reward**. By hijacking the cue, you can start a new behavioral chain.

---

# Example Habit Stacks (Personal Context)

| Existing Habit      | New Habit to Stack             | Resulting Stack                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Brush your teeth    | Floss one tooth                | "After I brush my teeth, I will floss one tooth"     |
| Make morning coffee | Write one line in a journal    | "After I make coffee, I will write one journal line" |
| Get into bed        | Read 2 pages of a book         | "After I get into bed, I will read 2 pages"          |
| Finish a meeting    | Log one highlight or task done | "After a meeting, I’ll log a task I completed"       |
| Turn off your alarm | Do 5 push-ups                  | "After turning off my alarm, I’ll do 5 push-ups"     |

---

# Steps to Use Habit Stacking

1. **List your current habits**: Focus on things you do daily and automatically. These form strong anchors.
2. **Pick a new habit that takes <2 minutes to start**: Start small to reduce friction. Scale it once it's established.
3. **Match based on context and location**: Stack habits in the same physical or temporal context (e.g. bathroom habit with another bathroom habit).
4. **Write the stack as a cue-behavior sentence**: Use: *“After \[anchor habit], I will \[new habit]”*
5. **Refine through iteration**: If you skip it or forget, the anchor may be weak. Replace or reposition it.

---

# Benefits
* **Leverages momentum:** You piggyback on habits you already do.
* **Reduces friction:** No new context or willpower required.
* **Creates routines:** Stacking can lead to full sequences (mini-routines).
* **Flexible scaling:** You can expand from one small habit to more.

---

# Common Mistakes
* **Stacking onto weak habits**: If the anchor isn't reliable, the new habit won't stick.
* **Too complex too soon**: Long chains collapse. Start with 1–2 steps max.
* **No environmental cue**: Make sure the trigger and action happen in the same place or situation.
* **Stacking unrelated or high-effort habits**: Keep transitions intuitive and small at first.

---

# Advanced Use: Habit Chains

Once your stack is reliable, you can chain more:

> “After I brew coffee, I’ll write one line in my journal. Then I’ll do one minute of deep breathing.”

Keep additions small and testable.
