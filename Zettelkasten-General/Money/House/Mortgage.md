A **mortgage** is a type of loan used to purchase real estate, where the property itself serves as collateral for the loan. The borrower agrees to repay the loan over time, typically through monthly payments, which include **principal** (the amount borrowed) and **interest** (the cost of borrowing).

# Key Components of a Mortgage
1. **Principal** – The original amount borrowed.
2. **Interest** – The cost of borrowing, usually expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR).
3. **Loan Term** – The length of time to repay the loan (e.g., 15, 20, or 30 years).
4. **Down Payment** – The upfront payment made by the buyer, usually a percentage of the home's price.
5. **Monthly Payments** – Regular payments that include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowner’s insurance, and sometimes private mortgage insurance (PMI).
6. **Collateral** – The home itself serves as security for the loan. If the borrower fails to make payments, the lender can **foreclose** (seize and sell the property).

# Types of Mortgages
- **Fixed-Rate Mortgage** – The interest rate remains the same for the entire loan term.
- **Adjustable-Rate Mortgage (ARM)** – The interest rate can change periodically based on market conditions.
- **FHA, VA, and USDA Loans** – Government-backed loans with specific eligibility requirements.
- **Jumbo Loan** – A mortgage that exceeds conforming loan limits set by regulators.
- A **buy-to-let (BTL) mortgage** is a type of mortgage designed for individuals who want to purchase a property to rent it out rather than live in it themselves. It differs from a standard residential mortgage in several ways, including deposit requirements, interest rates, and eligibility criteria.
# Amount of mortgage

The amount of mortgage you can get depends on several factors, including:
1. **Income** – Lenders assess your income to determine your ability to repay the loan. A higher income generally means you can qualify for a larger mortgage.
2. **Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI)** – Lenders compare your total monthly debt payments (including the new mortgage) to your gross monthly income. A lower DTI improves your chances of qualifying for a larger mortgage.
3. **Credit Score** – A higher credit score can qualify you for better interest rates and higher loan amounts. A lower score may limit your borrowing power.
4. **Down Payment** – A larger down payment reduces the amount you need to borrow and can help you qualify for a higher loan amount. Some lenders require a minimum down payment (e.g., 5%-20% of the property price).
5. **Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)** – This ratio compares the loan amount to the appraised value of the home. Lower LTV ratios (larger down payments) reduce lender risk and can increase the mortgage amount you qualify for.
6. **Employment History & Stability** – Steady employment and a consistent income source increase your credibility as a borrower. Self-employed individuals may need to provide extra documentation.
7. **Interest Rates** – Higher interest rates increase monthly payments, reducing the amount you can borrow based on affordability calculations.
8. **Property Type & Location** – Lenders consider the type of property (e.g., primary residence vs. investment property) and its location, as some areas or property types may carry higher risk.
9. **Loan Term** – The length of the loan (e.g., 15, 20, or 30 years) affects affordability and the total amount you can borrow.
10. **Lender Policies & Loan Programs** – Different lenders have varying criteria and loan programs, including government-backed loans (FHA, VA, USDA) that may have different borrowing limits.
