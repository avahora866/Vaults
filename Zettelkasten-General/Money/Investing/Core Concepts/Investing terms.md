# Basic Terms

- **Stock (Equity)**: 
	- A share of ownership in a company. When you own stock, you own a piece of that company.
	- Example: If you own one share of Apple, you’re a partial owner of Apple Inc.
-  **Bond**: 
	- A loan you give to a company or government in exchange for regular interest payments and repayment of the original amount at a later date.
	- Example: U.S. Treasury bonds are loans to the U.S. government.
- **Mutual Fund**:
    - A pool of money collected from many investors to invest in a diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other assets.
    - Managed by professionals, it’s a way to invest without picking individual stocks.
- **Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF)**:
	- Similar to a mutual fund but trades on the stock exchange like a stock. ETFs often track specific indexes, like the S&P 500.
- **Portfolio**:
	- Your collection of investments (stocks, bonds, funds, etc.).
    - Example: A portfolio might include 50% stocks, 30% bonds, and 20% real estate.

# Investment Strategies

- **Diversification**:
    - Spreading your investments across different assets to reduce risk.
    - Example: Instead of putting all your money into tech stocks, you diversify by investing in tech, healthcare, and energy.
- **Risk Tolerance**:
    - Your ability and willingness to endure the ups and downs of the market.
    - High-risk investments (like tech startups) offer high rewards but may lose money; low-risk investments (like bonds) are steadier.
- **Compound Interest**:
    - Earning interest on both the initial investment and the interest that has already been earned.
    - Example: If you invest $1,000 at 5% interest annually, you earn $50 in year one, and the next year, you earn interest on $1,050.

# Market Concepts

-  **Bull Market**:
    - A period when the stock market is rising, and investor confidence is high.
- . **Bear Market**:
    - A period when the stock market is declining, typically by 20% or more from recent highs.
- **Volatility**:
    - The degree to which the price of an investment fluctuates.
    - High volatility = big price swings. Low volatility = steadier prices.

---

# Financial Metrics

-  **Dividend**:
    - A portion of a company’s profits paid to shareholders, usually in cash or additional stock.
    - Example: If you own 100 shares of a company that pays $2 per share annually, you earn $200 in dividends.
- . **Market Capitalization (Market Cap)**:
    - The total value of a company’s outstanding shares. Calculated as: Share price × Number of shares.
    - Example: If a company’s share price is $50 and it has 1 million shares, its market cap is $50 million.
- **P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings Ratio)**:
    - A valuation metric that compares a company’s stock price to its earnings per share (EPS). It shows how much investors are willing to pay for $1 of earnings.
    - Example: A high P/E ratio could mean the stock is overvalued.
- **Yield**:
    - The income return on an investment, often expressed as a percentage.
    - Example: If a bond pays $50 annually on a $1,000 investment, the yield is 5%.

# Types of Orders

- **Market Order**:
    - An order to buy or sell a stock immediately at the current price.
- **Limit Order**:
    - An order to buy or sell a stock at a specific price or better.
    - Example: You set a limit order to buy a stock at $100, and it executes only if the price drops to $100 or lower.
- **Stop-Loss Order**:
    - An order to sell a stock if its price falls to a certain level, to limit potential losses.
    - Example: You set a stop-loss at $90 for a stock you bought at $100.

---

# Advanced Terms

- **Capital Gains**:
    - The profit made from selling an investment for more than its purchase price.
    - Example: If you buy a stock for $50 and sell it for $70, your capital gain is $20.
- **Asset Allocation**:
    - Dividing your investments among different categories like stocks, bonds, and cash to balance risk and reward.
- **Index Fund**:
    - A type of mutual fund or ETF designed to track the performance of a specific index, like the S&P 500.
- **Hedge**:
    - An investment made to reduce the risk of adverse price movements in another investment.
    - Example: Buying gold to hedge against a stock market downturn.
- **Leverage**:
    - Using borrowed money to increase the potential return of an investment. It increases both risk and reward.
- **Liquidity**:
    - How easily an investment can be converted into cash.
    - Example: Stocks are generally more liquid than real estate.
