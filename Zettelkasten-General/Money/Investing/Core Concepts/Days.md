The specific periods like **9-day**, **12-day**, **26-day**, **50-day**, or **200-day** for moving averages are **not randomly chosen**—they are based on a mix of:

# Market Convention and History

* These values became popular through decades of market use and technical analysis practices.
* Indicators like the **MACD** use 12-day and 26-day EMAs as defaults, influencing widespread adoption.
# Trading Timeframes

The "days" usually correspond to **trading days**, not calendar days:
* 1 week ≈ 5 trading days
* 1 month ≈ 21 trading days
* 1 quarter ≈ 63 trading days
* 1 year ≈ 252 trading days

So, typical moving averages correspond to these:

* **9-day / 10-day**: About 2 weeks – used for **short-term signals**
* **12-day / 26-day**: Common in **MACD** and momentum strategies
* **20-day / 21-day**: 1-month average – popular for **swing trading**
* **50-day**: Represents \~2.5 months – useful for **medium-term trends**
* **200-day**: \~1 year of trading – widely watched for **long-term trend reversals**

# Strategy Goals

* **Shorter periods** (e.g. 5–20 days): More responsive, good for quick trades, but noisier
* **Longer periods** (e.g. 50–200 days): Smoother, better for identifying sustained trends, but slower to react

# Backtesting and Optimization
Many traders **test** different period lengths on historical data to find what performs best for:
* A specific stock or asset
* A specific timeframe (e.g., intraday vs. daily)
* A particular market condition (trending vs. ranging)

# Summary

The chosen number of days in a moving average is influenced by:
* Standard trading calendars
* Historical use and convention
* Desired responsiveness vs. smoothness
* Strategy type (scalping, swing, long-term)
* Empirical testing and optimization
