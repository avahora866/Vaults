# Under Structure
## Line of Action
**The Line of Action** is a single, imaginary line that runs through the main axis of the character’s body. It dictates the primary movement and force of the pose.

**The Theory:** Think of the human spine. In a medical textbook, it is relatively rigid. In artistic theory, however, we treat the spine and the legs as a flexible, singular whip.
- **The "C" Curve:** The body bending forward or backward (e.g., a fetal position or an archer drawing a bow).
- **The "S" Curve:** The body twisting or shifting weight (e.g., a contrapposto standing pose or a dancer).

**The Rule of Connectivity:** For a dynamic pose, the Line of Action usually connects the **head** down through the **spine**, and flows directly into the **weight-bearing leg**, ending at the heel.

**Why your poses look stiff:** Stiffness occurs when artists draw the torso as a straight cylinder (like a soda can) and attach limbs to it. This creates a "snowman" effect. In contrast, the LOA requires you to ignore the anatomy initially and capture the _movement_.

**Statistical Reality:** In professional animation studios (like Disney or Ghibli), the "gesture drawing" phase often takes less than 30 seconds per figure. Yet, this 30-second sketch contains **80% of the visual information** regarding the character's intent. The remaining hours spent on anatomy and rendering only contribute the final 20% of clarity. You cannot detail your way out of a bad foundation.

## The Two-Mass Theory ("The Bean")
The most common reason for "stiff" torsos is treating the body as a single block. Anatomically, the human trunk is composed of two rigid masses connected by a flexible column.
1. **The Ribcage:** A large, air-filled bony cage. It cannot bend; it can only tilt and twist.
2. **The Pelvis:** A heavy, bone bowl. It also cannot bend.
3. **The Lumbar (Waist):** The only part that bends.

**The "Bean" Analogy** To prevent stiffness, we simplify the torso into a shape that looks like a kidney bean or a peanut.
- **The Stretch:** On the outside of the curve (the LOA side), the skin is taut and stretched.
- **The Pinch:** On the inside of the curve, the skin folds and compresses.

**Statistical Reality:** The human spine has approximately **80-90 degrees** of flexion (forward bending) and roughly **70 degrees** of extension (backward bending) in the cervical region, but the lumbar region (lower back) bears the brunt of the load in dynamic poses. When you draw a character bending, one side of the torso **must** be shorter than the other. If you draw the left and right sides of the waist with equal length, the character will look like a brick, not a living being.

**Practical Application:** When sketching, do not draw a rectangle for the body. Draw two ovals (ribcage and pelvis) overlapping slightly.
- If the character leans **forward**, the ovals overlap more (compression).
- If they lean **backward**, the ovals move apart (extension).
## Mannequinization (The Robo-Bean)
The "Bean" is excellent for gesture, but it is round and soft. It lacks structural integrity. To create a believable figure, you must convert these soft shapes into rigid, three-dimensional solids. This process is called **Mannequinization**.

We replace the organic shapes with geometric forms that have clear "Front," "Side," and "Top/Bottom" planes.

**The Forms:**
1. **The Ribcage:** Conceptualize this as an **Egg** or a rounded **Box**. It is rigid bone.
2. **The Pelvis:** Conceptualize this as a **Bucket** or a **Box**. It is also rigid bone.

**The Center Line (The Most Critical Line for Structure):** You must draw a line down the center of these forms (representing the sternum on the chest and the zipper line on the trousers).
- If the character turns left, the Center Line shifts left.
- If the character looks up, the Center Line curves up.

**Why your structure looks wrong:** Intermediate artists often draw the outline of the torso perfectly but fail to indicate the _orientation_ of the masses. They draw a front-view ribcage on top of a side-view pelvis. This creates a "broken" appearance.

**The "Robo-Bean" Technique:** Imagine the "Bean" you just learned, but flattened on the front and sides, like a box.
- This gives you **corners**.
- Corners tell the viewer exactly which way the body is facing.

**Statistical Reality:** The human ribcage is approximately **1.5 times the height** of the pelvis. However, the pelvis is generally **wider** (especially in female figures) or roughly equal (in male figures). Getting this ratio wrong is the primary cause of "long torso" or "short leg" syndrome.

## Primitives & Joints (The Connectors)
The biggest mistake intermediate artists make is "gluing" the arms and legs to the side of the body. In reality, limbs are **cylinders** that interlock with the torso.

**1. The Shoulder Girdle:** The arms do not attach to the ribcage directly; they attach to the **Clavicles** (collarbones) and **Scapula** (shoulder blades).
- Think of the shoulders as a **wire coat hanger** floating on top of the ribcage egg.
- This coat hanger can shrug up, slump down, or move forward/backward independently of the ribcage.
**2. The Limbs as Cylinders:** We construct arms and legs using simple cylinders.
- **Tapering:** The cylinders should not be perfect tubes. They should taper (get thinner) near the joints (wrist/ankle) and thicker near the muscle bellies.

**The Overlap Rule (Foreshortening):** To make the pose look like it occupies space, you must overlap the forms.
- If an arm is coming toward you, the **End** of the cylinder (the wrist) overlaps the **Beginning** of the cylinder (the elbow).
- **T-Junctions:** Where one line stops behind another, it creates a "T". This visual cue tells the brain "Shape A is in front of Shape B."

**Why your structure looks wrong (Part 2):** If you outline the arm as one continuous shape, it looks flat. If you draw the forearm cylinder _overlapping_ the bicep cylinder, you instantly create depth.

# Constructive Anatomy
## The Shoulder Girdle (The Pectoral-Deltoid Tie-In)
If your poses look stiff and the structure looks wrong, the "Armpit Area" is usually the culprit. In the mannequin phase, the arm is just a cylinder attached to a box. In anatomy, it is a complex intersection of three major systems: The **Clavicle**, the **Pectoralis Major**, and the **Deltoid**.

**The Architecture:** The arm does not just hang off the side; it is "capped" by the shoulder muscle.
1. **The Clavicle (Collarbone):** This is the handlebar. It runs from the center of the chest to the shoulder.
2. **The Pectoralis Major (Chest):** It originates from the sternum and clavicle. Here is the secret: The pectoral muscle **twists** and inserts _underneath_ the Deltoid muscle.
3. **The Deltoid (Shoulder):** This muscle acts like a shoulder pad. It originates on the spine and clavicle and inserts halfway down the arm.

**The Golden Rule of Layering:** The **Deltoid** is always on top.

**Visualizing the Insertion:** Imagine the Pectoral muscle is a piece of fabric coming from the chest. It does not stop at the armpit; it dives **under** the Deltoid to grab onto the arm bone (humerus).
- When the arm is raised, the Pectoral muscle stretches and un-twists.
- When the arm is down, the Pectoral muscle twists and bunches up.

**Why your structure looks wrong:** Many intermediate artists draw the chest muscle and the shoulder muscle touching side-by-side, or they draw the chest merging smoothly into the arm. This is incorrect. The Deltoid must visibly **overlap** the Pectoral muscle where they meet at the armpit. This overlap creates that necessary 3D depth.

## The Pelvis and Leg Attachment (The Great Trochanter)
The second most common cause of "stiff poses" is drawing legs that look like two pipes sticking straight out of the bottom of the torso.

To fix this, we must distinguish between the **"Waist"** and the **"Hips."**

**The Architecture:**
1. **Iliac Crest (The Waist):** This is the top rim of the pelvic bowl. When you put your hands on your hips, you are resting them here.
2. **The Great Trochanter (The Mechanical Hip):** This is the top of the leg bone (femur). It is located **lower** than the Iliac Crest.

**The Critical Structure:** The Femur (thigh bone) does not go straight down. It has a "neck" that protrudes **outward** sideways from the pelvis, hitting the Great Trochanter, and then the bone angles **inward** toward the knee.

**The "Underwear Line" (Inguinal Ligament):** Visually, the torso is separated from the legs by the Inguinal Ligament. This creates a "V" shape (like the line of a pair of briefs) at the crotch.
- **Mistake:** Intermediate artists often draw the side of the body as a straight line from ribcage to knee.
- **Correction:** The silhouette is a wave. It goes IN at the waist (Iliac Crest), OUT at the hip joint (Great Trochanter), and then tapers IN again toward the knee.

**Why your structure looks wrong:** If you draw the legs falling vertically from the width of the pelvis, the character looks boxy and weightless. In a natural standing position, the legs must taper inward to place the feet under the center of gravity (the Center Line we discussed in Module I).

## The Principle of Asymmetry (Straight vs. Curve)
A major hallmark of "stiff" art is **symmetry**. If you draw an arm or a leg where the left side mirrors the right side (like a cartoon bone `( )`), it looks static and manufactured. Organic things are **asymmetrical**.

**The Theory of Rhythm:** Muscles do not sit directly across from each other. They are staggered.
- To create a dynamic flow, you must pair a **Curved** line against a **Straight** (or straighter) line.

**The "Straight vs. Curve" Rule:**
1. **The Bone Side:** Usually the side of the limb where the bone is closest to the skin will differ from the muscular side.
2. **The Action:** The "Curve" represents the soft muscle doing the work. The "Straight" represents the tension or the bone providing support.

**Visualizing the Flow:** Imagine the eye traveling down the arm. It should not get stuck in "bulges." It should slalom like a skier.
- The curve of the deltoid pushes the eye **in**.
- The curve of the bicep pushes the eye **out**.
- The curve of the forearm pushes the eye **in** again.

**Practical Application (The Leg):** Let's look at the lower leg (from knee to ankle).
- **The Shin (Front):** This is mostly bone (tibia). It is hard and relatively **Straight**.
- **The Calf (Back):** This is muscle (gastrocnemius/soleus). It is soft and **Curved**.

If you draw both the front and back of the lower leg as curved lines `()`, the character looks like the Michelin Man. If you draw the shin straight and the calf curved `|)`, the leg looks sturdy and directional.

**Why your structure looks wrong:** You are likely drawing "sausage links"—stacking symmetrical ovals on top of each other. By offsetting the curves, you create a sense of gesture that runs _through_ the anatomy, rather than just outlining it.

# Line Economy & Hierarchy
## Line Weight as Information
A common trap for intermediate digital artists is letting the software smooth everything into a uniform, monoline "wire." This makes drawings look flat and manufactured.

In professional line art, the line is not just a boundary; it is a description of **Light** and **Gravity**.

**The Three Rules of Line Weight:**
1. **The Shadow Rule:** Think of your line as the deepest part of a shadow.
    - **Thick Lines:** Use them on the side of the form _away_ from the light source and where forms overlap (occlusion shadows).
    - **Thin Lines:** Use them on the side _facing_ the light source.
    - _Result:_ The drawing feels 3D before you even add colour.
2. **The Overlap Rule (T-Junctions):** When one object sits in front of another (e.g., the arm in front of the torso), the object **in front** gets a thicker outline. This pushes it forward visually.
3. **The Detail Rule:** Never use the same line weight for a wrinkle in a shirt that you use for the silhouette of the character.
    - **Silhouette:** Thickest (Container).
    - **Major Muscles/Clothing:** Medium.
    - **Surface Texture:** Thinnest (or broken lines).

**Practical Application in Clip Studio:** Do not just press harder. Be deliberate.
- **Underside of the chin:** Thick line (heavy shadow area).
- **Bridge of the nose:** Thin line (light hits it directly).
- **Armpit:** Thick line (deep crease).

**Why your structure looks wrong (Part 3):** If you outline the abs with the same thickness as the jawline, the abs will look like they are carved out of stone rather than sitting under the skin.

## The "Hairy Line" vs. Flow (Ghosting)
You mentioned stiffness. Stiffness often originates not just in the mind, but in the physical motion of the hand.

**The "Chicken Scratch" Syndrome:** Many intermediate artists sketch using short, repetitive, fuzzy strokes ("petting the line"). They draw a long curve by combining twenty small lines.
- **The Result:** The line looks nervous and messy. The energy of the gesture (LOA) is killed because the momentum is constantly starting and stopping.

**The Solution: Ghosting:** To capture the smooth "S" and "C" curves we discussed in Module I, you must execute long lines in a single stroke.
1. **Ghost the stroke:** Hover your stylus over the tablet. Move your hand in the shape of the line you want to draw _without_ touching the surface. Do this 2-3 times.
2. **Commit:** Once the motion feels right, lower the stylus and make the mark in one swift motion.
3. **Undo is free:** If you miss, Ctrl+Z and try again. Do not "fix" a bad line by scratching over it.

**The Pivot Point:** This is the physical mechanics of drawing.
- **The Wrist:** Good for small details (eyes, fingers). It has a limited range of motion (about 3-4 inches).
- **The Elbow/Shoulder:** Good for long lines (spine, legs, LOA).

**Why your poses look stiff (Final Reason):** If you draw a character's long back curve using your wrist, your hand physically cannot reach the end of the line without stopping and resetting. This breaks the curve. You must lock your wrist and swing from your elbow or shoulder.

# Value & Lighting Physics
## The Terminator & The Two Families
To render a character that looks like it exists in a real environment, you must understand how light wraps around a form.

**The Golden Rule:** Every object in your drawing belongs to one of two families:
1. **The Light Family:** Everything the light touches.
2. **The Shadow Family:** Everything the light cannot reach.

**The Terminator Line:** This is the border where the Light Family meets the Shadow Family. It is the most important line in rendering.
- On a **Hard** surface (like a box or a nose bridge), the Terminator is sharp.
- On a **Soft** surface (like a cheek or a thigh), the Terminator is soft and graduated.

**The Two Shadow Types:**
1. **Form Shadow:** The shadow _on_ the object itself, created because the form turns away from the light. (Example: The dark side of the moon).
2. **Cast Shadow:** The shadow the object _throws_ onto something else. (Example: The shadow of a building on the sidewalk).
    - _Crucial Note:_ Cast shadows are almost always **sharper** and **darker** than form shadows.

**Why your structure looks wrong (Rendering Edition):** A common mistake is "Pillow Shading"—airbrushing a fuzzy black shadow around the edges of everything. This makes the character look dirty or like a balloon. Real light has direction. The Terminator line proves that direction.

## Ambient Occlusion (The Crevice Shadow)
Often, structure fails because objects don't feel like they are touching each other. They feel like separate stickers layered in Photoshop. **Ambient Occlusion (AO)** is the glue that binds them.

**The Theory:** Light bounces around a room. It hits the floor, the walls, and your skin. However, there are tight crevices where light simply cannot squeeze into. These areas are the darkest points of your image.

**Where to find AO:** AO is not a "shadow" cast by a light source; it is a **lack of ambient light**.
- **Contact Points:** Where the feet touch the floor.
- **Deep Folds:** The crease of an armpit, the line between closed lips, the nostrils.
- **Overlap:** Where a tight shirt sleeve touches the skin.

**The Common Mistake (The "Black Hole" Effect):** Intermediate artists often make their shadows pitch black everywhere.
- **Correction:** Shadows are rarely black because of reflected light. Only the **AO**—the deepest cracks—should approach pure black.

**Practical Application in Clip Studio:** When colouring, use a specific layer (often set to "Multiply") just for these tiny, deep darks. This "pop" of contrast instantly adds weight and clarifies overlapping forms without muddying the whole drawing.

## Reflected Light (Bounce Light) & The Core Shadow
If you look at a photograph of a sphere (or a human face) in strong light, the shadow side is not one uniform flat grey. It glows slightly near the edge.

**The Physics:** Light rays travel in straight lines, but they bounce.
1. **Key Light:** Hits the object (Light Family).
2. **Bounce:** The light misses the object, hits the floor (or the character's red shirt), and bounces _back up_ into the shadow side of the chin or nose.

**The Core Shadow:** Because of this "Bounce Light," the darkest part of the shadow is actually **not at the edge**.
- The darkest part is a band _just after_ the Terminator line. This is called the **Core Shadow**.
- After the Core Shadow, the shadow gets _lighter_ again as it nears the edge of the object, due to the bounce light from the environment.

**The Value Hierarchy:**
1. **Highlight:** Brightest.
2. **Halftone:** The true colour of the skin in light.
3. **Core Shadow:** Darkest part of the shadow form (this creates the volume).
4. **Reflected Light:** Lighter than the core shadow, but **NEVER** as bright as the light family.

**Why your structure looks wrong:** If you make the edge of the shadow the darkest part, the object looks 2D. If you add a lighter rim (Reflected Light) on the shadow side, the brain instantly interprets the object as round because it sees the environment wrapping around it.

# colour Theory & Light Temperature
## The Hue Shift (Killing the "Muddy" Look)
The most common complaint from intermediate artists is that their colours look "muddy" or "dirty." This happens because they select a shadow colour by simply dragging the colour picker straight down towards Black.

**The Theory of Relativity:** In nature, shadows are not just _darker_; they are **more saturated** and a **different colour**.

**The Hue Shift Rule:** When choosing a shadow colour, you must do three things in your colour picker:
1. **Value:** Lower it (Darker).
2. **Saturation:** Increase it (Richer). _Shadows often hold more pigment than lit areas._
3. **Hue:** **SHIFT IT.** Do not stay on the same colour strip.

**Warm vs. Cool:**

- **Sunlight (Warm):** If your light source is yellow/orange (Warm), your shadows should generally shift towards the **Cool** spectrum (Purples, Blues, Cool Reds).
- **Overcast/Moonlight (Cool):** If your light source is blue (Cool), your shadows should shift towards the **Warm** spectrum (Oranges, Warm Browns).

**Practical Application (The "Skin" Recipe):** If your base skin tone is a pale **Orange**:
- _Don't_ just pick a dark Orange for the shadow. It will look like brown dirt.
- _Do_ shift the hue slider toward **Red** or **Purple** (Cooler/Richer) and increase saturation. This creates the effect of blood flowing under the skin.

**Why your art looks "muddy":** Black desaturates colour. By adding black to orange, you get a dull, lifeless grey-brown. By shifting orange to red/purple, you maintain life and vibrancy even in the darks.

## Subsurface Scattering (The "Glow")
You are drawing people, not mannequins. Human skin is not opaque like stone; it is translucent like wax or a gummy bear.

**The Physics:** When light hits skin, it doesn't just bounce off. Some light penetrates the surface, travels through the flesh, hits the blood vessels underneath, turns **Red**, and then exits the skin again.

**Where to see it (The "SSS" Zones):**
1. **The Terminator Line:** Right at the edge of the shadow on the face (the transition zone), you should paint a thin band of highly saturated **orange or red**. This shows the light scattering just under the surface before it gets lost in the shadow.
2. **Thin Body Parts:** Ears, nostrils, and fingertips. If a bright light is behind a character, their ears should glow bright red/orange.

**The "Hard" vs. "Soft" Edge:**
- **Without SSS:** The transition from Light to Dark looks dry and hard.
- **With SSS:** That saturated red band softens the look of the skin, making it feel fleshy and alive.

**Practical Application:** In Clip Studio Paint, you can often achieve this by taking a soft airbrush with a saturated orange/red colour and lightly glazing the edge of your shadows on the skin.
