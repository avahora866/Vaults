From a drawing perspective, the muscular system creates the volume and form of the figure. If the skeleton is the rigid armature, the muscles are the elastic volumes stretched over it.

# Origin and Insertion
This is the most important mechanical concept for an artist. Muscles are like elastic bands that pull; they cannot push.
- Origin: The anchor point of the muscle. It attaches to the bone that moves less (e.t., the humerus or upper arm bone for the biceps).
- Insertion: The moving point. It attaches to the bone that is pulled (e.g., the radius in the forearm for the biceps).

Why this matters: When a muscle contracts (activates), it pulls the insertion point toward the origin point. This makes the muscle "belly" (the main body) become shorter, wider, and firmer.
This creates antagonistic pairs. When one muscle contracts (the agonist), its opposite relaxes and stretches (the antagonist).

Example: When you flex your elbow, your biceps (agonist) contracts and bunches up. Your triceps (antagonist) on the back of the arm stretches and flattens. When you straighten your arm, the roles reverse.

# Simplification and Form
Muscles are 3D volumes. To draw them, simplify their complex shapes into basic forms. They rarely look like individual strings; they create larger, flowing masses.

Think of muscles in simple geometric shapes:
- Spindle/Teardrop: Biceps, calf muscles.
- Fan: Pectorals (chest), latissimus dorsi (back).
- Strap: Neck muscles (sternocleidomastoid), sartorius (thigh).
- Sheet: Abdominals, external obliques.

When drawing, focus on how these forms overlap and interlock. For example, the deltoid (shoulder) muscle overlaps the biceps and triceps, and the external obliques weave into the serratus anterior (the "finger" muscles on the ribs).

# Major Muscle Groups for Drawing
Focus on the large, superficial muscles that define the figure's silhouette and surface forms.

Neck:
- Sternocleidomastoid (SCM): Two thick straps forming a 'V' from the skull (behind the ears) to the clavicle and sternum.

Torso (Front):
- Pectoralis Major: The "pecs." A fan shape from the clavicle and sternum to the upper arm.
- Rectus Abdominis: The "abs." A sheet of muscle from the lower ribs to the pubic bone, divided by tendons.
- External Obliques: The "love handles." Sheet muscles on the side of the torso, connecting ribs to the iliac crest (hip bone).

Torso (Back):
- Trapezius: A large, diamond-shaped muscle covering the upper back and neck.
- Latissimus Dorsi: The "lats." Large fan-shaped muscles of the mid-back that wrap around and tuck into the armpits.
- Erector Spinae: Two columns of muscle running alongside the spine.

Shoulder:
- Deltoid: The "shoulder cap." A triangle-shaped muscle that gives the shoulder its round form.

Arm:
- Biceps Brachii: The "biceps." A two-headed muscle on the front of the upper arm.
- Triceps Brachii: The "triceps." A three-headed muscle on the back of the upper arm.
- Forearm Group (Extensors/Flexors): A complex group. Simplify it into two main masses: the "flexor mass" on the inside (thumb-side) of the elbow and the "extensor mass" on the outside.

Leg:
- Gluteus Maximus: The "glutes." The large muscle of the buttock.
- Quadriceps Femoris: The "quads." The large group of four muscles on the front of the thigh.
- Hamstrings: The group of muscles on the back of the thigh.
- Gastrocnemius: The "calf." The two-headed, diamond-shaped muscle on the back of the lower leg, which connects to the Achilles tendon.
- Tibialis Anterior: The shin muscle, which flexes the foot upward.
