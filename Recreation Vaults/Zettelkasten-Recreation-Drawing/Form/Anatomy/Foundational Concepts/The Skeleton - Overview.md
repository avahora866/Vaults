Think of it in three main parts:

1. Major Masses: Simplify the complex skeleton into simple, 3D forms.
2. Bony Landmarks: Identify the key points where bone is close to the skin.
3. Articulation: Understand how the major parts connect and move.

# Major Masses
To draw the skeleton from imagination or in different poses, simplify the three main parts of the torso and head.
- The Skull: Treat it as a sphere for the cranium (back) and a separate, flatter block for the face and jaw.
- The Ribcage: Simplify this into an egg or barrel shape. It is rigid and provides the form for the upper torso.
- The Pelvis: Visualize this as a tilted bucket or a flattened box. It is also rigid and forms the base of the torso, connecting the spine to the legs.

The spine connects these three rigid masses. It is a flexible, 'S'-shaped column. The relationship between the tilt of the skull, ribcage, and pelvis defines the gesture and balance of the entire figure.

# Bony Landmarks
Landmarks are your most important reference points. They are parts of the skeleton close to the surface that are visible (or create visible forms) regardless of muscle or fat. You use these points to build the figure's structure and check proportions.

Key landmarks to find first include:

- Clavicles (Collarbones): These 'handlebar' shapes connect the ribcage to the arms.
- Acromion Process: The bony tip of the shoulder.
- Scapula (Shoulder Blade): Specifically, the inner edge and bottom tip, which are very prominent in many back poses.
- Sternum (Breastbone): The center-line of the ribcage.
- Iliac Crest: The 'hip bones' you can feel at your waist. This defines the top edge of the pelvis.
- Greater Trochanter: The wide, bony point on the outside of your upper leg (femur). It's the widest point of the hips.
- Patella (Kneecap): The obvious bone in the center of the knee.
- Malleolus (Ankle Bones): The inner and outer bony bumps of the ankle. The outer one (fibula) sits lower than the inner one (tibia).

# Proportion and Articulation

The skeleton sets the proportions of the figure. A common artistic convention is the "8-head" model, where the total height of the figure is eight times the height of the skull.

- Head 1: Top of head to chin.
- Head 2: Chin to nipples.
- Head 3: Nipples to navel.
- Head 4: Navel to pubic bone (bottom of the pelvis). This is the halfway point.
- Head 5: Pubic bone to mid-thigh.
- Head 6: Mid-thigh to just below the knee.
- Head 7: Below knee to mid-shin.
- Head 8: Mid-shin to sole of the foot.

Finally, understand the joints, which are the points of articulation. Focus on their type and range of motion.

- Ball-and-Socket (Shoulder, Hip): Allows wide rotation in all directions.
- Hinge (Elbow, Knee): Allows movement in only one direction (flexion and extension).
- Pivot (Neck, Forearm): Allows for twisting or rotation.
