In figure drawing, simplifying the human body into basic three-dimensional forms is a foundational technique. This approach, sometimes called "mannequinization," allows you to establish accurate proportions, create a sense of volume, and construct dynamic poses before adding anatomical details. By breaking down the complex structures of the body into manageable shapes, you can better understand how they move and interact in space.

### **The Three Main Masses**
A common starting point is to visualize the three main masses of the torso: the **head**, the **ribcage**, and the **pelvis**. These can be represented by simple forms that help to establish the overall gesture and balance of the figure.
- **The Head:** Often simplified as an **oval** or an **egg shape**. It can also be conceptualized as a sphere with the sides sliced off and a jawline added.
- **The Ribcage:** Typically drawn as a larger, **egg-like shape** or a **barrel**. It's important to consider its three-dimensional form, as it tilts and rotates.
- **The Pelvis:** Can be simplified as a **flattened box**, a **bowl**, or a **wedge shape**. This helps to define the angle of the hips and their relationship to the ribcage.
### **Connecting the Masses and Adding Limbs**
Once the three main masses are in place, you can connect them and add the limbs using even simpler forms.
- **The Spine:** The spine is a crucial element that dictates the flow and gesture of the pose. It can be represented by a **curved line** connecting the ribcage and the pelvis. The degree of curvature will determine the dynamism of the pose.
- **The Limbs:** The arms and legs can be simplified into a series of **cylinders** or **tapered cylinders**. This helps to create a sense of perspective and foreshortening.
- **The Joints:** The major joints of the body—the shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles—can be represented by **spheres**. This allows you to visualize the range of motion and the way the limbs articulate.

### **Putting It All Together**
The process of constructing a figure using simplified forms generally follows these steps:

1. **Gesture Drawing:** Start with a quick gesture drawing to capture the overall movement and energy of the pose. This is often just a few flowing lines.
2. **Establish the Main Masses:** Block in the head, ribcage, and pelvis using simple 3D forms. Pay attention to their relative sizes and angles.
3. **Connect with the Spine:** Draw the line of the spine to connect the ribcage and pelvis, reinforcing the gesture.
4. **Add the Limbs and Joints:** Use cylinders for the limbs and spheres for the joints to build out the arms and legs.
5. **Refine the Contour:** Once the underlying structure is established, you can begin to refine the outlines, adding the more subtle curves and anatomical details.

By starting with these simplified forms, you create a solid foundation for a more detailed and realistic figure drawing. This method encourages you to think about the body in three dimensions and helps to avoid the common pitfall of simply copying outlines.
