### Perspective in Drawing

Perspective in drawing refers to the technique used to represent three-dimensional objects on a two-dimensional surface (like paper or a canvas) to create the illusion of depth and space. Mastering perspective is essential for creating realistic drawings of the world around you. Here’s a breakdown of the key concepts:

#### 1. Horizon Line

The horizon line is a fundamental element in perspective. It represents the viewer's eye level in the scene. In a landscape or cityscape, it’s where the sky meets the ground (or sea).

- **Position**: The position of the horizon line determines the viewer’s point of view. For example:
	- High horizon line: This implies that the viewer is looking downward at the scene. Essentially, the viewer is positioned higher up in space, such as on a hill, in a tall building, or on a plane, looking down.
	- Low horizon line: This suggests that the viewer is looking upward at the scene. The viewer’s eye level is closer to the ground, like if they were standing on a low-lying area or looking up at a tall structure.

- **Relation to vanishing points**: The horizon line is also where vanishing points typically appear in linear perspective (more on this later).


#### 2. Vanishing Points

Vanishing points are the points where parallel lines appear to converge in the distance. They guide the direction in which lines recede and disappear. There are several types of perspective based on the number of vanishing points:

- **One-point perspective**: All lines converge to a single vanishing point on the horizon line. This is often used for scenes viewed directly from the front, like a road stretching off into the distance or a hallway.
    
- **Two-point perspective**: Lines converge toward two vanishing points, typically used for scenes where the viewer is looking at a corner of a building or an object at an angle.
    
- **Three-point perspective**: Three vanishing points are used—two on the horizon line and one either above or below the horizon, typically used for depicting tall objects (like skyscrapers) or when the viewpoint is extreme (looking up or down).
    

#### 3. Types of Lines in Perspective

- **Orthogonal lines**: These are the lines that converge at the vanishing point(s). They are typically the edges of objects or surfaces in space.
- **Transversal lines**: These are lines that remain parallel to the picture plane, such as the width of a floor or the spacing between columns, and don't converge at the vanishing points.

#### 4. The Picture Plane

The picture plane is the flat surface (paper or canvas) where the scene is depicted. The space behind the picture plane is the three-dimensional world you're representing. The horizon line and vanishing points are based on the viewer's relationship to this plane.

#### 5. Atmospheric Perspective

Atmospheric perspective refers to the way objects appear less distinct and lighter in color as they recede into the distance. This mimics how the atmosphere reduces contrast and color saturation, enhancing the illusion of depth.

#### 6. Key Tips for Drawing in Perspective

- **Start with the horizon line**: Always begin by deciding where the horizon line will go, as it sets the stage for the rest of the drawing.
- **Use vanishing points**: Determine where your parallel lines will converge, depending on your perspective type.
- **Practice basic shapes**: Practice drawing basic shapes (squares, rectangles, circles) in perspective. This helps you understand how objects change when viewed from different angles.
