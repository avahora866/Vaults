**Ingredient:** Flour (Wheat)
**(Optional) Scientific Name:** *Triticum aestivum* (Common Wheat)

# Summary
A powder made by grinding wheat grains, providing the primary structure for baked goods through gluten development, and also used for thickening sauces and coating foods.

# Flavor and Aroma
* **Flavor Profile:** Largely neutral, slightly sweet or nutty depending on the variety (whole wheat is earthier). Primarily acts as a carrier for other flavors.
* **Aroma Profile:** Raw: Dusty, neutral, subtly grassy (especially whole wheat). Cooked/Baked: Develops nutty, toasted aromas due to Maillard reactions and caramelization.
* **Key Compounds:**
    * **Starch** (Amylose and Amylopectin): Provides bulk, absorbs liquid, and gelatinizes upon heating to set structure.
    * **Proteins** (primarily **Glutenin** and **Gliadin**): When hydrated and agitated (mixed/kneaded), these proteins form **Gluten**, an elastic network that traps gases and provides structure, elasticity, and chewiness to baked goods. Protein content varies by flour type.
    * **Fiber, Lipids, Minerals:** Present in varying amounts, especially higher in whole wheat flour which includes the bran and germ.

# Culinary Function
* **Primary Role:** Providing **structure** to baked goods (bread, cakes, pastries), **thickening** liquids (roux, slurries), **binding** ingredients, and providing a **coating** for fried foods.
* **Effect of Heat:** Starches absorb water and gelatinize (swell and set). Proteins (gluten) coagulate and firm up, setting the final structure. The surface browns through Maillard reactions and caramelization.
* **Interactions:**
    * **Hydration:** Mixing with water/liquid allows gluten proteins to link and form the gluten network. The amount of liquid affects consistency (dough vs. batter).
    * **Mixing/Kneading:** Develops the gluten network, aligning the proteins to create strength and elasticity (essential for bread). Overmixing can make products tough (e.g., cakes, muffins).
    * **Fat:** Coats gluten strands, shortening them and inhibiting network formation, resulting in a more tender, less chewy product (e.g., shortcrust pastry, cakes).
    * **Sugar:** Competes with starch and protein for water, which tenderizes the product and slows gluten development. Also contributes to browning.

# Common Forms (Wheat Flour)
Based primarily on protein content, affecting gluten potential:
* **Plain Flour / All-Purpose Flour:** Medium protein (~10-12%). Versatile for cakes, cookies, quick breads, pastries, thickening sauces.
* **Bread Flour / Strong Flour:** High protein (~12-14%). Develops strong gluten. Ideal for yeast-leavened breads that require a chewy texture and strong structure.
* **Cake Flour / Soft Flour:** Low protein (~7-9%). Develops weak gluten. Results in a tender, fine crumb structure ideal for delicate cakes (e.g., sponge, chiffon) and pastries.
* **Self-Raising Flour:** Plain flour with added chemical leavening agents (usually baking powder) and salt. Used for convenience in recipes where chemical leavening is desired (e.g., scones, some cakes, quick breads).
* **Whole Wheat Flour / Wholemeal Flour:** Made from the entire wheat kernel (bran, germ, endosperm). Higher in fiber and nutrients, nuttier flavor, produces denser, heavier baked goods. Bran particles can interfere with gluten development.
* **Semolina / Durum Flour:** High protein flour from durum wheat, typically coarser grind. Primarily used for pasta, couscous, and some Mediterranean/Indian breads and puddings.
* **"00" Flour (Italian):** Finely milled flour, protein content varies. Often used for pasta and pizza dough for its texture.

*(Note: Many other flours exist from grains like rye, barley, corn (maize), rice, oats, and non-grains like almonds, coconut, potato, tapioca. These have different properties, often lacking gluten.)*

# Preparation
* **Measuring:** Accuracy is key in baking. **Weight (grams)** is the most accurate method. If using volume, the **spoon-and-level method** (fluff flour, spoon lightly into cup, level off with a straight edge) is preferred over scooping, which compacts the flour.
* **Sifting:** Aerates flour, removes lumps, and helps blend dry ingredients evenly. Often recommended for lighter cakes and pastries.
* **Mixing:** Combining with liquids and other ingredients to form doughs or batters. Method and duration depend on desired gluten development (minimal for cakes, extensive for bread).
* **Roux:** Cooking flour in fat (usually butter) as a base for thickening sauces (béchamel, velouté, gravy). Removes raw flour taste and prevents lumps.

# Pairings and Cuisine
* **Classic Pairings:** Butter, sugar, eggs, salt, water, milk, yeast, baking powder/soda (fundamental baking ingredients).
* **Protein Pairings:** Used as a coating for fried chicken/fish, binder in meatballs/meatloaf, base for pasta served with meat/cheese, bread served with anything.
* **Spice Pairings:** Virtually any, depending on the application (e.g., cinnamon, nutmeg for sweet; herbs, pepper for savory).
* **Common Cuisines:** Universal. Base ingredient for breads, pasta, noodles, cakes, pastries, cookies, dumplings, tortillas, and countless other staples worldwide. Used to thicken gravies, sauces, and stews in many cuisines.

# Substitutions
* **General:** Substituting different types of *wheat* flour affects texture (e.g., using bread flour for cake flour makes a tougher cake). Substituting *non-wheat* flours is complex, especially in baking, due to the absence of gluten.
* **For Thickening:** Cornflour (cornstarch), arrowroot, potato starch, tapioca starch (use less, creates glossier finish). Rice flour.
* **Gluten-Free Baking:** Requires specific blends of gluten-free flours (e.g., rice, almond, oat, buckwheat) and starches, often with binders like xanthan gum, tailored to the specific baked good. Single gluten-free flours rarely substitute 1:1 for wheat flour.
* **Self-Raising Flour:** For 1 cup (approx. 125-150g) of self-raising, use 1 cup plain flour + 1.5-2 teaspoons baking powder + 1/4 teaspoon salt.
