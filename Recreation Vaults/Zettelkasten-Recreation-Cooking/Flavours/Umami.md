Umami is the fifth basic taste, alongside sweet, sour, salty, and bitter.

- **Definition:** The term "umami" is Japanese and translates to "pleasant savory taste" or "essence of deliciousness." It is often described as a **savory**, **meaty**, or **broth-like** flavor that spreads across the tongue, lasts longer than other basic tastes, and causes a mouthwatering sensation.
    
- **Source:** The umami taste is triggered by specific chemical compounds acting on dedicated taste receptors on the tongue. The main compounds are:
    
    - **Glutamate:** An amino acid, most commonly found in the form of its salt, **Monosodium Glutamate (MSG)**. Glutamate is abundant in proteins.
        
    - **Inosinate** (or Inosine Monophosphate, IMP): Mainly found in **meat** and **fish**.
        
    - **Guanylate** (or Guanosine Monophosphate, GMP): Mainly found in **plants**, especially **mushrooms**.
        
- **Mechanism:** These compounds are generally breakdown products of protein and nucleic acids, which is why umami flavor is enhanced in foods that are **cooked**, **aged**, or **fermented**. The combination of glutamate with inosinate or guanylate creates a strong **synergistic effect**, boosting the umami taste significantly more than either compound alone.
    
- **Significance:** Umami signals to your body that the food contains protein, an essential nutrient. This response helps saliva and digestive juices get ready to process the food.
    
- **Examples of Umami-Rich Foods:**
    
    - **Aged Cheeses:** Like Parmesan (Parmigiano Reggiano)
        
    - **Cured Meats:** Such as ham and bacon
        
    - **Fermented Products:** Including soy sauce, miso paste, and kimchi
        
    - **Vegetables:** Ripe tomatoes, mushrooms (especially dried shiitake), and seaweed (like kombu)
        
    - **Broths and Stock:** Especially those made from meat, fish, or seaweed
