Social psychology is the study of how people’s thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are influenced by the actual, imagined, or implied presence of others. In other words, it looks at how being around other people—or even thinking about them—changes what we do, what we think, and how we feel.

# Concepts
## Conformity => Linked Note
Conformity occurs when people adjust their behavior, attitudes, or beliefs to match those of a group. This can happen even if the group is clearly wrong or if you privately disagree. People conform for many reasons: to fit in, avoid conflict, or gain approval. Everyday examples include laughing at a joke you don’t find funny or following fashion trends because everyone else does. Studying conformity helps explain peer pressure and why social norms are so powerful.

There are two main types:
- Normative Conformity: Changing behavior to gain social approval or avoid rejection. For example, laughing at a joke you don’t find funny just because everyone else is laughing.
- Informational Conformity: Changing behavior because you believe the group has better information. For example, if everyone in a room looks confused by a speaker, you might also assume you misunderstood something.

A classic experiment is Asch’s line study: participants were asked to match line lengths. When everyone else gave the wrong answer, many participants went along with it, even though it was obviously wrong.

Conformity shows how social pressure shapes decisions and behavior, often subconsciously. It explains why people follow trends, join popular opinions, or act differently in groups than alone.

## Obedience
Obedience is when someone follows instructions or commands from an authority figure, even if the action goes against their personal beliefs. It explains why ordinary people can perform harmful actions when directed by someone in power. Obedience explains behaviors in structured settings like workplaces, schools, and the military, and why people sometimes act against their moral judgment under authority pressure.

A famous example is Milgram’s experiment. Participants were instructed to give what they thought were electric shocks to another person whenever they answered a question incorrectly. Even though many felt uncomfortable, a large number continued giving shocks when prompted by the experimenter. This showed that ordinary people could perform actions they might normally consider wrong if an authority figure tells them to.

Factors that increase obedience:
- Authority legitimacy: People obey more when the authority figure seems official or credible.
- Proximity of authority: Closer or physically present authorities increase obedience.
- Victim distance: People are more likely to obey if they don’t see the person being affected.
- Gradual escalation: Small requests first, followed by larger ones, make people more likely to comply (the “foot-in-the-door” effect).

Factors that decrease obedience
- Presence of dissenters: If others refuse to obey, people are more likely to resist.
- Personal responsibility: When people feel responsible for consequences, they obey less.
- Questioning legitimacy: Doubting the authority’s right to command can reduce obedience.

### Physcology Mechanisms
#### Agentic state
When people enter an agentic state, they stop seeing themselves as fully responsible for their actions. Instead, they see themselves as instruments carrying out someone else’s instructions. For example, in Milgram’s experiment, participants often said, “I’m just doing what the experimenter told me.” This mental shift reduces guilt or hesitation because responsibility is perceived as belonging to the authority figure.

#### Moral conflict
Even when obeying, people often feel a clash between what they are told to do and what they personally believe is right. This creates stress, discomfort, or hesitation. Some people experience high tension and refuse to continue, while others find ways to justify their actions to reduce the conflict. Moral conflict shows that obedience is not always easy or automatic—our conscience still plays a role.

## Social Influence & Persuasion => Linked Note
Social influence is the way people are affected by others’ presence or opinions, while persuasion is a more deliberate attempt to change beliefs or behavior. Techniques include appealing to emotions, using social proof (everyone else is doing it), or invoking authority. Persuasion appears everywhere—from advertising to politics—and helps explain why people adopt certain ideas, make purchases, or vote in particular ways. Unlike obedience, it doesn’t require a direct command from an authority figure. Instead, it often works subtly through suggestion, norms, or appealing to emotions.

### Social Influence 
This is the broad effect that other people have on your behavior, consciously or unconsciously. It includes:
- Normative influence: Acting a certain way to fit in or be accepted by a group.
- Informational influence: Changing your behavior or opinion because you think others have better knowledge or insight.

### Persuasion
Persuasion is a more deliberate attempt to change someone’s attitude or behavior. Techniques often studied in psychology include:
- Reciprocity: People feel obligated to return favors.
- Social proof: People follow the crowd (“everyone else is doing it”).
- Authority: People are influenced by credible experts.
- Commitment/Consistency: People like to act consistently with past statements or actions.
- Liking: People are more likely to be influenced by someone they like.
- Scarcity: Limited availability increases perceived value or urgency.

## Attitudes & Beliefs => Linked Note
Attitudes are evaluations people hold about objects, ideas, or people, which influence how they behave. Beliefs are the cognitive part—what we think is true. Together, attitudes and beliefs guide decisions and interactions. For example, someone who believes exercise is healthy (belief) and enjoys it (attitude) is more likely to work out regularly. Studying these shows how internal thoughts affect outward behavior.

### Beliefs
Beliefs are what we think is true about the world, people, or situations. For example, believing that exercise is healthy or that studying improves your skills. Beliefs are cognitive—they shape how we interpret information and make decisions.

### Attitudes
Attitudes are evaluations or feelings we hold toward something or someone—positive, negative, or neutral. For example, you might enjoy exercise (positive attitude) or dislike loud parties (negative attitude). Attitudes influence behavior and motivation.

### Connection to behavior
Beliefs and attitudes together affect how people act. For example, if someone believes in climate change (belief) and feels strongly about protecting the environment (attitude), they are more likely to recycle or support eco-friendly policies.

### Relevance
Studying attitudes and beliefs explains why people can resist or accept social influence, why persuasion works, and why behavior sometimes conflicts with expressed opinions.

## Social Perception & Attribution => Linked Note
Social perception is how we form impressions of others, and attribution is how we explain their behavior. People often attribute others’ actions to personality traits (internal) or situational factors (external). For example, if someone is late, you might think they are careless (internal) or stuck in traffic (external). Understanding attribution helps explain misunderstandings, blame, and empathy.

### Social Perception
This is the process of forming impressions of others. It includes noticing body language, tone of voice, facial expressions, and other cues. Our perceptions are influenced by our expectations, past experiences, and stereotypes. For example, you might assume someone is unfriendly if they avoid eye contact, even if they’re just shy.

### Attribution
Attribution is how we explain someone’s behavior. Psychologists divide it into two main types:
- Internal (dispositional) attribution: Believing the behavior comes from the person’s character or personality. Example: “He failed the test because he’s lazy.”
- External (situational) attribution: Believing the behavior is caused by external factors. Example: “He failed the test because it was unusually difficult.”

## Stereotypes, Prejudice & Discrimination
Stereotypes are generalized beliefs about groups, prejudice is an emotional attitude toward a group, and discrimination is behavior based on those beliefs or feelings. These concepts explain biases in everyday life, from hiring decisions to social interactions. Studying them helps reveal why people treat others unfairly and how to reduce such bias.

### Stereotypes
Stereotypes are oversimplified or generalized beliefs about a group of people. For example, thinking “all teenagers are reckless” is a stereotype. They can be positive, negative, or neutral, but they simplify complex human behavior and often ignore individual differences.

### Prejudice
Prejudice is an emotional or attitudinal response toward a group, usually based on stereotypes. It’s a judgment or feeling—like dislike, fear, or admiration—directed at members of a group, often without personal experience.

### Discrimination
Discrimination is behavior that treats people unfairly because of their group membership. It’s the action side of prejudice, like refusing to hire someone because of their gender or ethnicity.

## Group Behavior
Group behavior studies how people act differently in groups than alone.

- Groupthink: When a group values harmony and consensus so much that it overlooks problems or poor decisions. For example, a team might agree on a risky plan without questioning it because no one wants to create conflict.
- Deindividuation: Loss of self-awareness and restraint in groups, often leading to behavior people wouldn’t normally do alone. For example, people may act aggressively at a sports event or during a protest because the group provides anonymity.
- Bystander Effect: People are less likely to help someone in need when others are present, often assuming someone else will act.

## Interpersonal Relationships => Linked Note
This area looks at how people form and maintain friendships, romantic relationships, and cooperative bonds. It explores attraction, trust, communication, conflict, and cooperation. Understanding relationships explains why some connections thrive while others fail and how social bonds affect emotions and behavior.

### Key aspects of interpersonal relationships:
- Attraction: Factors that make people drawn to each other, such as physical appearance, similarity in interests, or proximity.
- Trust and Communication: Sharing information honestly and reliably strengthens bonds. Misunderstandings or broken trust can damage relationships.
- Conflict and Cooperation: All relationships face conflicts; how people handle disagreements—through negotiation, compromise, or avoidance—affects relationship quality.
- Attachment and Support: Emotional support and secure attachments contribute to strong, lasting relationships, while insecure attachments can lead to problems or instability.

## Social Identity
Social identity is the part of a person’s self-concept derived from group memberships. People act in ways that align with their social groups, and group identity can influence behavior, loyalty, and prejudice. This concept explains behaviors like nationalism, team loyalty, or solidarity with a community.

### Key aspects of social identity:
- In-group vs. Out-group: People tend to favor their own group (in-group) and may hold biases or negative attitudes toward others (out-group). This can influence cooperation, competition, and prejudice.
- Group norms and behavior: Being part of a group often shapes how individuals behave, what they value, and what they consider acceptable. People often conform to group expectations to maintain belonging.
- Self-esteem and group membership: Identifying with a group can boost self-esteem and provide a sense of belonging, security, and purpose.

## Aggression & Prosocial Behavior
Aggression is behavior intended to harm others, while prosocial behavior is behavior intended to help. Factors influencing these behaviors include biology, environment, social learning, and context. Understanding them helps explain why people sometimes act violently or helpfully, and what conditions encourage cooperation or conflict.

### Aggression
Aggression is behavior intended to harm or injure another person, physically or psychologically. Causes include biological factors (like genetics or hormones), environmental triggers (stress, frustration), social learning (observing aggressive models), and situational factors (crowds, anonymity). Examples range from arguments escalating to violence, to road rage or bullying.

### Prosocial Behavior
Prosocial behavior is any action intended to benefit others, such as helping, sharing, or cooperating. It is influenced by empathy, moral reasoning, social norms, and situational cues (like whether others are present or if the person feels responsible). Everyday examples include helping someone with directions, donating to charity, or volunteering.
