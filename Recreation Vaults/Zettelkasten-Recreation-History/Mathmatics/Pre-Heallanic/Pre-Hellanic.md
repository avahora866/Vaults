Pre-Hellanic referes to the time period of 3000 BCE – 600 BCE. 
"Pre-Hellenic" literally means "before the Hellenes" (the Greeks). It refers to the period and the civilizations that existed before classical Ancient Greek (Hellenic) culture rose to prominence

This category primarily includes two groups:
- Babylonian (Mesopotamian) Mathematics: Known for their base-60 number system, clay tablets with multiplication tables, and methods for solving quadratic equations.
- Egyptian Mathematics: Known for their base-10 system, practical geometry for surveying land (especially after the Nile's floods), and their use of unit fractions.

The key distinction is that Pre-Hellenic mathematics was almost entirely practical. It was a tool for solving concrete problems: building, accounting, surveying, and astronomy for calendars.

The "Hellenic" period that followed is considered a new era because the Greeks (starting around 600 BCE) were the first to begin studying mathematics for its own sake. They introduced the concepts of formal proof, axioms, and abstract logic, shifting the focus from "how to calculate this" to "why is this true?"

## Babylonions
The Babylonians (Mesopotamians) had a sophisticated and abstract mathematical system.
- Number System: Their primary advancement was the sexagesimal (base-60) number system. We still use this system today for measuring time (60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour) and angles (360 degrees in a circle).
- Algebra: They were highly skilled in algebra. Cuneiform tablets show they could solve linear, quadratic, and even some cubic equations.
- Computation: They used "lookup tables" on clay tablets for multiplication, division, squares, and square roots, allowing them to perform complex calculations.
- Geometry: They understood the relationship between the sides of a right triangle long before the Greeks. The famous tablet Plimpton 322 is a list of Pythagorean triples, demonstrating this knowledge.

## Egyptions
Egyptian mathematics was highly practical, driven by the needs of construction, taxation, and administration.
- Number System: They used a base-10 (decimal) system. It was written in hieroglyphs and was additive, not positional (the symbol for 100 written five times meant 500).
- Fractions: Their most distinct feature was the use of unit fractions. They expressed all fractions as the sum of fractions with a numerator of 1 (e.g., they would write 2/5 as 1/3 + 1/15). The Rhind Papyrus is a key document showing these calculations.
- Geometry: They were masters of practical geometry. They had accurate formulas for the areas of triangles, rectangles, and circles.
- Volumes: Their most impressive achievement was calculating the volume of a truncated pyramid (a pyramid with the top cut off). This was essential for their massive construction projects.
