# Number System
The Babylonian number system was one of the first and most advanced of the ancient world.

The Babylonians used a sexagesimal system, which is a base-60 system. We still use this today for measuring time (60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour) and for angles (360 degrees in a circle).

The number 60 is highly versatile because it is divisible by many numbers (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60), which made calculations with fractions much simpler than in a base-10 system.

The most critical feature of the Babylonian system was that it was positional.  In our base-10 positional system, the number 222 means:
$$(2 \times 10^2) + (2 \times 10^1) + (2 \times 10^0)$ or $(2 \times 100) + (2 \times 10) + (2 \times 1)$$
In the Babylonian base-60 system, the number (represented as 2, 2, 2) would mean:
$(2 \times 60^2) + (2 \times 60^1) + (2 \times 60^0)$ or $(2 \times 3600) + (2 \times 60) + (2 \times 1) = 7200 + 120 + 2 = 7322$.
The value of a symbol depended on its position in the number.

## The Cuneiform Symbols
The Babylonians did not have 59 different symbols. Instead, they used just two cuneiform symbols pressed into clay:
- The Wedge (𒁹): Represented the number 1.
- The Angle Bracket (𒌋): Represented the number 10.

They used these two symbols in an additive way to build the numbers from 1 to 59. These groups of symbols would then be placed in different positional columns.

Example: To write the number 23, they would combine two angle brackets (20) and three wedges (3):𒌋𒌋𒁹𒁹𒁹 = 23
To write a larger number, like 85, they would use the positional system. 
85 = (1 $\times$ 60) + 25
So they would write the symbol for 1, leave a space, and then write the symbol for 25: 𒁹 (space) 𒌋𒌋𒁹𒁹𒁹𒁹𒁹

## The Problem of Zero
For over a thousand years, the Babylonian system had a major ambiguity: it lacked a symbol for zero. 

They had no way to represent an empty place. The number 7202 (which is $2 \times 3600 + 0 \times 60 + 2 \times 1$) would be written as (2) (space) (2). This could be confusing. How could you tell (2) (2) (which is $2 \times 60 + 2 = 122$) from (2) (space) (2) (which is 7202)?

It also meant the symbols for 1, 60 ($1 \times 60$), and 3600 ($1 \times 60^2$) were all identical (a single wedge 𒁹). The value had to be determined from the context of the problem.

Later in the Seleucid period (around 300 BCE), scribes began using a placeholder symbol (two slanted wedges) to mark an empty internal position, like the '0' in our number 305. However, they did not use it at the end of a number, so the ambiguity of 2 versus 120 (2 $\times$ 60) remained.

# Algebra
Babylonian algebra was highly advanced for its time. It was not symbolic like our modern algebra (no $x$ or $y$), but was instead rhetorical, meaning problems and solutions were written out in words as procedures.

## Types of Equations
Babylonians developed algorithmic methods for solving problems that, in modern terms, are equivalent to:
- Linear equations: Such as $ax = b$.
- Systems of linear equations: Such as $x + y = S$ and $x - y = D$.
- Quadratic equations: Such as $x^2 + bx = c$ and $x^2 - bx = c$.
- Some cubic equations: Specifically forms like $x^3 + x^2 = c$.

## The Method: Completing the Square
The most significant achievement was their method for solving quadratic equations. They did not have a "quadratic formula," but they had a step-by-step algorithm that is identical to the modern method of completing the square. They often thought about these problems in geometric terms. A problem like "a square's area plus its side equals a number" ($x^2 + x = c$) was visualized as a geometric square and a rectangle.

Example Problem
A common type of problem found on a cuneiform tablet might read (in translation): I added the area and the side of my square, and it is 0;45. [This is $x^2 + x = 0.75$]
The scribe would then follow a procedure:
1. Take half of the coefficient of the "side" (x): Half of 1 is 0.5.
2. Square this value: $(0.5)^2 = 0.25$.
3. Add this to the original number: $0.75 + 0.25 = 1$.
4. Take the square root of the result: $\sqrt{1} = 1$.
5. Subtract the value from step 1: $1 - 0.5 = 0.5$.

The "side" (our $x$) is 0.5.

This is precisely the algorithm for completing the square. The Babylonians had mastered this procedure 3,000 years before it was formalized in Europe.

## Use of Tables
This kind of algebra was only possible because of their computational tools. To solve these problems, scribes would constantly refer to clay tablets containing:
- Multiplication tables
- Tables of reciprocals (for division)
- Tables of squares ($n^2$) and cubes ($n^3$)
- Tables of square and cube roots
When the procedure said "take the square root of the result," the scribe would find the number on their "square root" tablet to get the answer.
