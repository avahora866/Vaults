#TRUNK

### What the Spiral Actually Is

Before a framework can work, you need to recognise what's happening. The spiral isn't reflection — it's a loop with four components running simultaneously:

- **Replay** — re-running the moment, usually inaccurately
- **Magnification** — the awkward 3 seconds becomes the defining moment of the whole interaction
- **Projection** — assuming you know what the other person thought/felt
- **Forecasting** — concluding this will have lasting consequences

Knowing which of these is dominant in a given spiral tells you what tool to reach for.

---

### The Framework: Four Stages

#### Stage 1 — Don't Process Yet (0–2 hours after)

The spiral feels urgent. It isn't. Your emotional state immediately after a bad interaction makes you a completely unreliable narrator of what actually happened. The assessment you make in this window is almost always more negative than reality.

**What to do instead:**
- Name it, don't engage it. *"I'm spiralling about that interaction. I'm not going to solve this right now."*
- Physical interruption — move rooms, make something, go outside. The goal isn't distraction as avoidance; it's interrupting the loop long enough to let the initial heat drop.
- Set a deliberate processing window. Not "I'll think about it later" — actually decide: *"I'll give this 15 minutes tonight."* This tricks the anxious brain into releasing it temporarily because it knows there's a scheduled slot.

---

#### Stage 2 — Fact Separation

When you do process, start by splitting the event from your interpretation.

Draw a line down the page (mental or literal):

| What actually happened | What I'm telling myself about it |
|---|---|
| I made a joke and no one laughed | They all think I'm annoying |
| There was a long silence after I spoke | I killed the conversation |
| I got someone's name wrong | They'll never trust me |

The left column should contain only things a camera would have captured. The right column is your narrative. Most of the spiral lives in the right column, and most of it isn't evidence — it's hypothesis.

---

#### Stage 3 — The Three Questions

Once you've separated fact from interpretation, run the interpretation through these:

**1. Would an objective observer agree?**
Not a generous friend, not a harsh critic — someone neutral. What would they actually conclude from the facts column?

**2. What's the most boring explanation?**
Anxious minds reach for the most self-implicating interpretation. But most social awkwardness has a boring explanation: they were distracted, they were tired, they misheard, they were thinking about something else. The boring explanation is usually more likely.

**3. How much of this is about me vs. how much is about them?**
People are mostly focused on themselves. The moment that felt catastrophic to you was probably a blip to them — if it registered at all.

---

#### Stage 4 — The Repair Decision

After you've done the above, you'll usually land in one of three places:

**A — Nothing to repair**
The event was smaller than the spiral made it. This is the most common outcome. The work here is giving yourself explicit permission to close the file — not "I'll try to stop thinking about it" but *"I've processed this and I'm done."* Many people skip this step and the loop keeps running on low heat.

**B — Something small to repair**
A follow-up message, a brief acknowledgement next time you see the person, or just adjusting your approach next time. Keep the repair proportionate to the actual offence — over-apologising or over-explaining can make something awkward worse. One clean action, then close the file.

**C — Something worth learning**
There was a real mistake and something to genuinely take forward. Extract it specifically and non-punitively: *"I interrupted too much — next time I'll notice that sooner."* Not *"I'm bad at conversation."* The learning has to be actionable and specific, or it just becomes self-criticism with a bow on it.

---

### The Reset

The spiral often continues because there's no signal that processing is over. Build one.

This sounds minor but isn't. It can be anything: writing the thing down and closing the notebook, saying out loud *"done,"* going for a walk and returning. The function is to give your brain a clear endpoint, because anxiety loops partly because there's no defined completion.

---

### A Note on Frequency

If the spiral is happening after most interactions, the framework above is still useful — but the deeper work is on the **underlying belief** generating the spirals, not the individual events. Common culprits: *"I'm being constantly evaluated,"* *"social mistakes have lasting consequences,"* *"other people don't make these errors."* These are worth examining directly, because no recovery framework fully compensates for a belief system that keeps generating the problem.
