#TRUNK

## What this is about

Talking about yourself involves a different set of mechanics than expressing ideas about the external world. It requires deciding what to share, at what depth, without either performing or hiding. The skills are buildable — but they're mostly downstream of self-knowledge, not communication technique.

---

## The core reframe: performing vs hiding isn't a binary

The goal isn't zero performance. All self-disclosure is curated to some degree — you choose words, sequence, emphasis. Trying to be "completely unperformed" tends to produce its own kind of performance (the conspicuously authentic person).

The real target is: **curated but honest** — not uncurated.

---

## Deciding what to share

Three filters worth running before or during a conversation:

### 1. Does this serve the conversation, or does it serve me?

The practical test: if you imagine not sharing the thing, who feels the loss?

- If the loss is that *they won't know something about you* — that's serving yourself.
- If the loss is that *the conversation would be missing something* — that's serving the conversation.

A real-time signal: are you listening, or are you waiting? If something they said triggered a thought about you and you're now holding it, half-listening, waiting for a gap — that's usually for you. Things that genuinely serve the conversation tend to arise at the moment they're relevant, not get queued.

### 2. Is this the right register for this relationship?

Register is mostly about reciprocity and pacing, not a fixed rule about relationship stage.

Signals that depth is welcome:
- They've gone to similar depth themselves, unprompted
- They're asking follow-up questions rather than moving through topics
- The conversation has slowed — there's space rather than momentum
- There's some privacy (not surrounded by others, not mid-task)

The asymmetry: going too deep too fast is more costly than staying shallower than you could. Oversharing makes people feel responsible for something they didn't ask to hold. But chronically staying shallow when depth is available is its own cost — it's just slower, and it's mostly paid by you.

A simple rule: match or go one notch beyond what they've offered. Not two.

### 3. Am I being asked, or am I inserting?

This is the most concrete of the three — it gives you a decision rule rather than a judgment call.

Being asked doesn't only mean a direct question. Someone sharing something vulnerable about themselves is also an invitation. That counts.

## At what depth

Common errors:

- **Too abstract**: "I find it hard to trust people" — this reports a conclusion but gives nothing to work with. Specific is better: "I notice I tend to wait for someone to let me down before I relax around them."
- **Too much context**: Over-explaining origins and motivations signals anxiety about being misread rather than genuine explanation.
- **Avoiding uncertainty**: "I think I..." and "I'm not sure if this is actually true but..." aren't hedges — they're accuracy. You don't have perfect access to yourself, and pretending otherwise closes the conversation.

---

## Common failure modes

### Performing looks like:
- Saying the version of the thing that sounds more interesting than the actual thing
- Describing yourself from the outside ("I'm someone who...") rather than from the inside ("I find that I...")
- Pre-empting judgment by framing yourself first ("I know this sounds weird, but...")
- Making self-disclosure subtly flattering — the struggles imply depth, the quirks are the charming kind

The tell: *would I say this exactly the same way if I were certain they'd think worse of me for it?* If no, it's performing.

### Hiding looks like:
- Humour as deflection at the moment when something real surfaces
- Staying in the third person or the general case ("people tend to find that..." / "you know how sometimes...")
- Describing *what happened* rather than *what you made of it*
- Answering the adjacent question rather than the actual one

---

## The core principle

**Speak from inside the experience rather than about it from outside.**

"About" keeps you safe. "From inside" is where the actual thing is.

- Outside: "I found it difficult."
- Inside: "I kept second-guessing whether I was reading it right."

The second is riskier and more accurate simultaneously. That's usually the sign you've found the right level.

## The personal problem: scrambling and blurting

The scrambling and blurting that happens when asked about yourself is usually less a communication problem and more an **access problem**.

When someone asks you about yourself, there's a brief internal scan. If nothing is pre-processed and ready, you grab whatever's nearest the surface — which tends to be random, anxiety-shaped, or whatever you think they wants to hear.

Blurting specifically is interesting: it's not too little coming out, it's too much, unfiltered. That suggests there's plenty to say — just no structure to reach for when the pressure's on.

Two things that help:
1. Doing the thinking before you're in the situation — not scripting, but having spent enough time with your own experience that there's something organised to access.
2. The pause is underrated. "Let me think about that" buys five seconds and signals you're taking it seriously. Most people experience that as more engaging, not less.

Worth checking: is the difficulty actually general, or does it depend on context? Most people find they're better at talking about themselves in some situations — a particular person, an anchored topic, a slower-paced setting. If there are exceptions, those are worth noticing. They tell you what the real obstacle is.

## The roadmap

Six stages, progressing only when the previous one feels comfortable. Move on when the current stage feels natural rather than forced.

### Stage 1 — Written, solo (no audience)
- Answer "who am I?" in writing — just to see what comes out
- Take a vague self-description and rewrite it as something concrete
- Write about a recent situation where you felt strongly — what did you actually feel, not what you think you should have felt
- Describe yourself to an imaginary stranger in 5 sentences, then mark anything that sounds like performance

*Move on when writing about yourself feels natural rather than forced.*

### Stage 2 — Written, with a receiver (async — you have time to think)
- Reply to a "how are you" text with something more honest than your usual answer
- Send one thing you've been thinking about to a friend you wouldn't normally mention
- When asked for your opinion, give the actual one rather than hedging
- Write a message sharing something you found difficult — and send it without softening it into nothing

*Move on when you can send something personal without editing the realness out of it.*

### Stage 3 — Spoken, anchored (with one trusted person, topic-led)
- Talk about a film, book, or event and include what it actually made you feel — not just what you thought of it
- When something comes up that connects to your experience, share the connection rather than holding it
- Practice the pause — when asked something about yourself, say "let me think" and actually think
- Notice when you use humour or deflection to avoid the real thing — just notice, don't force a change yet

*Move on when you can share something real without immediately deflecting or filling the silence.*

### Stage 4 — Spoken, direct questions (still with trusted people)
- When asked directly about yourself, answer the actual question rather than the easier adjacent one
- Let yourself be the subject for a few minutes without redirecting to the other person
- After an answer that came out wrong, try rephrasing: "actually what I meant was..."
- Notice the difference between answering from inside the experience vs describing it from outside

*Move on when blurting has reduced — when answers feel chosen rather than grabbed.*

### Stage 5 — Less familiar contexts (new-ish people, broader settings)
- Use the "being asked vs inserting" check — only share about yourself when a door is genuinely open
- Practice matching register — share one notch deeper than the other person, no more
- In a group setting, make one contribution that's genuinely yours rather than a safe take
- When you blurt something, note what triggered it — pressure, silence, wanting to impress?

*Move on when you can calibrate depth to the person without overthinking it.*

### Stage 6 — Open, cold situations ("tell me about yourself" territory)
- Prepare 2–3 things from your personal inventory you can reach for when asked cold — not scripts, just anchors
- In a new professional or social context, share one thing that's actually true rather than the expected answer
- When the question is too broad, narrow it yourself: "That's a big one — the thing most on my mind lately is..."
- Afterwards, reflect: what did I say, what did I mean to say, and where was the gap?

*This is the hardest stage. The earlier stages are what make it possible.*

---

## Parallel track — running throughout all stages

This runs alongside the roadmap, not after it. It's more important than it looks — it's how you build the internal library that makes the roadmap stages actually work.

**Weekly writing practice**
Write 3 specific true things about yourself each week. Convert vague self-descriptions into concrete ones: "I'm impatient" becomes "I find it hard to wait when I feel like I already understand the situation."

**Post-conversation reflection**
After conversations, ask: did I say what I actually meant, or the safer version of it?

**Build a personal inventory**
A running note of things you know to be true about yourself, with one concrete example each. This is what you'll draw on when the pressure's on and nothing feels available.

**Notice when it's easier**
Pay attention to when talking about yourself feels more natural — who you're with, the setting, what you're doing. Those exceptions tell you something important about what the real obstacle is.

---

## The underlying logic

The skill of talking about yourself is mostly built in reverse order to how it feels: you don't practise speaking until you have something to say, and you don't have something to say until you've done the inner work of knowing yourself with some precision. The parallel track is the foundation. The stages are the practice. The principle — speak from inside rather than about — is the compass.
