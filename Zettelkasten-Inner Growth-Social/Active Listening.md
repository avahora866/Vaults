#TRUNK

## The Core Distinction: Inward vs Outward

Most social confidence work is **inward-facing** — managing anxiety, expressing needs, not people-pleasing. What's often missing is the **outward-facing** side: making the other person feel heard, asking questions that open rather than interrogate, and reading when someone wants to leave or go deeper.

Social confidence isn't just about what you output — it's also about making the other person comfortable. That skill is learnable.

---

## 1. Making Someone Feel Genuinely Heard

There's a difference between *listening* and *demonstrating you've listened*.

**Reflecting back — not parroting.** Mirror the substance, not the words. Not "so you hate your job" but "sounds like it's less the work and more feeling invisible there." This tells them you processed what they said, not just waited for your turn.

**Naming what's underneath the words.** People often say the surface thing and mean the deeper thing. "Work's been busy" often means "I'm overwhelmed and nobody's noticed." Gently going one layer deeper — "that sounds exhausting, not just logistically" — makes people feel seen at the right level. Don't over-psychologise; just occasionally go one level down.

**Sitting with something before pivoting.** A common habit is to receive what someone said and immediately redirect to your own story or a solution. Staying on their thing for one more beat — a follow-up question, "tell me more about that" — signals it actually landed.

---

## 2. Questions That Open Rather Than Interrogate

The difference is **specificity + invitation vs demand**.

- "What do you do?" → interrogation (closed, evaluative)
- "What are you working on at the moment that's interesting you?" → invitation (open, non-hierarchical)

**Good questions tend to:**
- Assume something positive ("what's been good about it" rather than "do you like it")
- Give the person an escape hatch — they can answer at whatever depth they want
- Follow *their* thread, not introduce a new one of yours

**Rule:** After someone answers, your next question must come from *what they just said*, not from your own mental list. This feels completely different to the receiver.

**One question at a time.** Stacking questions creates pressure and feels like an interview. Ask one, then wait.

---

## 3. Reading When Someone Wants to Leave or Go Deeper

**Exit signals:**
- Shorter answers, less elaboration
- Eyes drifting, body turning slightly away
- Wrapping-up language ("yeah, totally", "anyway…")
- Glancing at phone or watch without doing anything with it

The move isn't to hold them — it's to give them a graceful exit before they have to manufacture one. Saying "I'll let you get back to it" at the right moment leaves a better impression than more conversation would.

**Depth signals:**
- They answer and ask you the same question back
- They elaborate beyond what you asked — adding backstory, context
- They lean in, make more eye contact
- They self-disclose something slightly vulnerable

When you see depth signals: slow down, don't rush to fill space, let silence sit. It creates room for the conversation to deepen naturally.

---

## 4. The Curiosity Problem

The issue isn't a lack of curiosity about someone's topic (e.g. insurance) — it's trying to be curious about the *subject* rather than the *person*. These are completely different things.

Nobody is interesting because of their job category. But almost everyone is interesting if you get underneath it:

- What drew them to it vs what they expected
- What genuinely frustrates them that outsiders don't see
- What they're quietly proud of that nobody ever asks about
- Whether they actually care about it or it's just what happened

"What's the most misunderstood thing about working in insurance?" is infinitely more interesting than anything about insurance itself — because now you're getting their actual perspective, not a job description.

**Topics matter less than people think.** You can have a dull conversation about travel and a fascinating one about parking enforcement. The curiosity problem dissolves when you stop trying to be interested in *what they do* and start being interested in *what it's like to be them*.

---

## 5. Why Work Dominates (and How to Move Beyond It)

Work feels "safe" — but it's not really safe, it's **scripted**. There are established moves: ask what they do, show interest, share what you do, compare notes. Nobody has to reveal anything.

The discomfort with other topics isn't danger — it's that the script runs out and someone has to actually offer something real. That requires mild vulnerability from at least one person, and usually whoever goes first.

**The way out isn't a topic switch — it's a depth shift.** You don't have to abandon work. You just go somewhere work doesn't usually go:

- "Is this what you thought you'd be doing?"
- "Do you actually like it, or is it just what happened?"
- "What would you do if you weren't doing this?"

These are still technically about work but they ask about the person underneath. From there it branches naturally.

**Self-disclosure.** Someone has to go first — and it doesn't have to be them. Offering something genuine about yourself — not heavy, just real — gives the other person permission to do the same. Said lightly, something like "I've been thinking about whether I actually like what I do or just got used to it" tends to pull honest answers out of people because you've shown it's allowed.

Work stays dominant in conversations because both people are implicitly agreeing to stay there. It only takes one person to quietly shift the level.

## 6. The Active Listening Roadmap

A six-phase practice. The phases are roughly sequential but you'll cycle back — treat each as "where to focus now" rather than something you complete and leave behind.

---

### Phase 1 — Notice your own habits
*Before changing anything, just observe.*

- After a conversation, ask: how often did I redirect to my own story?
- Notice when you're forming your reply while the other person is still talking
- Count how many questions you asked vs statements you made

**Milestone:** You can accurately describe your default habits — whether you tend to redirect, solve, advise, or fill silence.

---

### Phase 2 — Stay on their thread
*Stop redirecting; follow what they said.*

- Every follow-up question must come from something they just said — not your own list
- When you feel the urge to share your own story, pause and ask one more question first
- Let a silence sit for 2 seconds before jumping in

**Milestone:** You can sustain 4–5 exchanges entirely on their topic without steering toward yourself.

---

### Phase 3 — Demonstrate you've heard
*Reflect back — not parroting, but distilling.*

- Before responding, briefly mirror the substance: "so the issue is less the workload and more feeling unsupported"
- Occasionally name the feeling underneath the facts: "that sounds exhausting, not just logistically"
- Check your reflection: "is that what you mean, or is it more…?"

**Milestone:** People noticeably relax or elaborate after you reflect — a "yeah, exactly" or them going deeper is the signal it landed.

---

### Phase 4 — Ask questions that open
*Invite rather than interrogate.*

- Replace "do you like it?" with "what's been interesting about it lately?"
- Ask one question, then wait — never stack questions
- Try opinion/reaction questions: "what surprised you about that?" or "is that what you expected?"

**Milestone:** Your questions consistently produce more than a one-sentence answer — people elaborate without being prompted again.

---

### Phase 5 — Read the signals
*Notice when someone wants to leave or go deeper.*

- Watch for exit signals and offer the exit before they have to manufacture one
- Watch for depth signals: they ask you the same question back, they self-disclose slightly, they elaborate beyond what you asked
- When someone goes deeper, slow down — resist filling space

**Milestone:** You end conversations at the right moment and deepen them when the signal is there.

---

### Phase 6 — Offer something real yourself
*Self-disclosure makes reciprocity possible.*

- Share a genuine opinion or uncertainty once per conversation — something small but real
- Let what they said actually land before pivoting
- Notice when a conversation becomes mutual — both people disclosing, both following each other's threads

**Milestone:** Conversations become mutual without effort — both people are curious, both following, neither performing.

---

## Where to Start

Given the curiosity problem, starting at **Phase 1** is more useful than trying to perform curiosity you don't feel yet. Watch yourself in real conversations for a week without trying to change anything. You'll start noticing the exact moment you disengage — and that moment is what's worth fixing.

**Phase 3 is where the "boring topic" problem dissolves.** You don't need to care about insurance. You need to care enough to distil what they actually said about it. That's a much smaller ask.

---

*The underlying principle across all of this: move your attention from yourself to them. Most social anxiety is self-focused — how am I coming across, what do I say next. Genuine curiosity about what it's like to be the other person naturally produces most of these behaviours. The techniques are just scaffolding while that shift is still developing.*
