#TRUNK
## The Technique

The exercise I want to try: pick a topic, write freely with pen and paper, follow thoughts wherever they lead, jump between topics as naturally as they come, keep going until the thoughts are exhausted.

This is called **free writing**, and when aimed at self-understanding it overlaps with what researchers call **expressive writing**.

---

## The Research

The key researcher is psychologist **James Pennebaker** (work dating from the 1980s onward).

**Key findings:**
- Writing about thoughts and feelings for just 15–20 minutes over 3–4 days produced measurable improvements in psychological wellbeing and even physical health
- The mechanism: writing forces you to **structure and label** experiences that were previously just noise — creating distance and a sense of control
- Venting alone is not enough. **Reflective writing** — making sense of things — was more effective than pure emotional dumping

**Important nuance:** People who only wrote about negative emotions without shifting toward meaning-making got *less* benefit. The pivot from *describing* to *interpreting* is where self-knowledge actually forms. Occasionally ask: **why does this matter to me?**

---

## Following Associations Matters

Staying on topic is not the goal. The **associative jumps** — where the mind drifts when thinking about something else — are often the most revealing part. What you drift *to* tells you what's actually connected and what you actually care about.

**Pen and paper over typing** is a reasonable choice — the slower pace encourages more deliberate, reflective thought rather than reactive thought.

---

## Choosing Topics

The best topics have **personal emotional charge**. They put *you* in the picture, not just your intellect. If the writing could have been written by a stranger, the topic isn't personal enough yet.

**Strong topic choices:**

- Things you want but feel uncomfortable admitting
- A decision you're currently avoiding
- What genuinely angers or frustrates you *(anger reliably signals values — you only get angry when something you care about is being violated)*
- What a good life looks like to you — not what it *should* look like, what actually pulls at you
- Someone you envy, and why *(envy is uncomfortable but specific — it points directly at what you want)*
- A time you felt most like yourself — or the opposite, a time you felt like you were performing

**Subjects like "science" are a weaker starting point** because they produce thoughts about the subject rather than about yourself. They can work if used as a *doorway* — e.g. starting with *why* science interests you, what specifically draws you in, whether that interest feels freely chosen, what you'd do with it if nothing was in the way — and getting personal quickly.

---

## Core Principle to Keep in Mind

> Describe → then Interpret. Don't just write what you think or feel — occasionally ask yourself *why* it matters to you. That's where the self-knowledge forms.
