#TRUNK

## What people pleasing actually is

People pleasing is a **coping mechanism**, not a personality trait. It typically stems from one of three roots:

- Fear of conflict
- Fear of rejection
- A deep-seated belief that your needs matter less than other people's

It's important to distinguish people pleasing from **empathy**. They look identical from the outside but feel completely different internally. Empathy is a genuine strength — caring about how others feel. People pleasing is performing care to avoid discomfort for yourself. Knowing which is which matters so you don't overcorrect and become unnecessarily blunt.

---

## Core principles to keep returning to

**Their feelings are not your responsibility.**
You can care about someone without managing their emotional state. These are different things.

**Discomfort is the skill, not assertiveness.**
Most advice tells you to "just be more assertive." That's the output. The actual training is learning to tolerate the discomfort of someone being disappointed or annoyed with you. You build that tolerance gradually — you don't suddenly become a different person.

**Some people will push back.**
People in your life who have come to rely on your compliance will resist when you change. That resistance is not a sign you're doing something wrong. It's a sign the change is real.

**The goal is not to stop caring — it's to stop managing other people's feelings at the expense of your own.**

---

## Practical techniques

| Technique | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Buy time | "Let me get back to you" — even for small things |
| Say the real thing quietly | "I'd rather not" / "that doesn't work for me" — no justification needed |
| Drop the pre-emptive apology | Don't soften the no before saying it — the no is enough |
| Log what you held back | Notice in real time when you agreed but didn't mean it |
| Stop explaining yourself | An explanation invites negotiation. A calm no does not. |

---

## The six-stage roadmap

Work through these in order. Only move on when a stage feels **automatic**, not just possible.

### Stage 1 — Buy yourself time
**Default:** "Let me get back to you" before any yes or no, even on small things.
**Goal:** Break the automatic yes reflex. Create a gap between the stimulus (a request) and your response.

### Stage 2 — Sit with discomfort
Let someone be mildly disappointed or inconvenienced. Notice that you survive it. Don't fix it, don't apologise, don't follow up — just let it be.
**Goal:** Learn to separate their feelings from your responsibility. This is the foundational skill everything else is built on.

### Stage 3 — Notice what you're not saying
Keep a mental (or written) log of moments where you held back an honest opinion, agreed when you didn't, or softened something you meant. Don't try to change yet — just observe.
**Goal:** Build real-time awareness of the pattern. You can't change what you can't see.

### Stage 4 — Say the real thing, quietly
Start expressing honest preferences and mild disagreements. You don't need to be confrontational. "I'd rather not" and "that doesn't work for me" said calmly is enough. No justification required.
**Goal:** Honest output, without needing the other person to be okay with it.

### Stage 5 — Drop the pre-emptive apology
People pleasers often apologise *before* the no to soften it. Stop. Let the no stand on its own. You're not doing anything wrong by having a preference.
**Goal:** Stop treating the other person's reaction as your fault before it's even happened.

### Stage 6 — Hold firm under pushback
When someone resists your boundary — pushes back, guilt-trips, goes quiet — don't fold, over-explain, or immediately backtrack. Hold the position.
**Goal:** Reframe pushback. It is proof the change is working, not proof you are wrong.

---

## Red flags to watch for in yourself

- Saying yes and immediately feeling resentment
- Apologising before stating a preference or a no
- Giving long explanations to justify a simple decision
- Checking in repeatedly to make sure the other person is still okay with something
- Feeling responsible when someone is in a bad mood around you
- Agreeing in the moment and quietly undoing it later
- Avoiding sharing your actual opinion "to keep the peace"

---

## A note on identity

People pleasing often feels like kindness from the inside. That's what makes it hard to let go of. But there's a difference between being genuinely kind and performing compliance to manage anxiety. The former costs you nothing. The latter costs you yourself — slowly, and usually invisibly.

Changing this isn't about becoming selfish or cold. It's about becoming honest. The people who matter will adjust. The people who only valued your compliance will struggle — and that tells you something important too.

---

*Start with Stage 1. Stay there until it feels boring. Then move on.*
