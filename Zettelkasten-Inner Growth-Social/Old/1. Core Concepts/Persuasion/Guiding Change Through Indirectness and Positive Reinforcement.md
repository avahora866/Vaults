Effective persuasion begins with a soft approach, focusing on praise, indirect feedback, and fostering a collaborative environment, rather than direct criticism.

* **Begin with Praise (The "Novocain" Approach):**
    * Start difficult conversations or feedback sessions with genuine praise. This is compared to a dentist using Novocain: the patient still gets the drilling, but the pain is reduced, making them more receptive.
* **Call Attention to Mistakes Indirectly:**
    * This method works wonders, especially with sensitive people who might resent direct criticism.
    * *Example:* A dance teacher successfully encouraged improvement by praising the student's fundamentals and minimizing errors, rather than emphasizing mistakes like a previous teacher had done.
* **Acknowledge Your Own Mistakes:**
    * It's much easier for someone to hear about their faults if the criticizer humbly admits their own imperfections first. This disarms defensiveness.
* **Phrase Orders as Questions:**
    * Asking questions makes an order more palatable and often stimulates the creativity of the person being asked. People are more likely to accept an order if they had a part in the decision.
* **Use Praise Instead of Criticism (B.F. Skinner's Principle):**
    * This fundamental concept shows that when criticism is minimized and praise emphasized, good behaviors are reinforced, and poorer ones atrophy from lack of attention.
* **Praise the Desired Trait (Act "As If"):**
    * If you want to improve a person in a certain aspect, act as though that particular trait were already one of their outstanding characteristics.
    * *Example:* A manager wanted to re-motivate a mechanic whose work quality had declined. Instead of criticizing, the manager praised the mechanic's "reputation for turning out first-class work," leading the mechanic to live up to that praise and regain his previous skill.
* **Minimize Errors:**
    * When giving feedback, focus on praising what someone does right and minimize their errors to encourage positive change without discouraging them.
* **Make Them Happy to Comply:**
    * Always ensure the other person is happy about doing the thing you suggest. This encourages willing cooperation.
    * *Example:* Colonel House, experienced in human relations, always followed this rule to successfully influence others.
* **Allow Them to "Save Face":**
    * Preserve the other person's dignity, especially if they've made a mistake or are in a difficult situation. Destroying their "face" can ruin a working relationship permanently.
    * *Example:* A supervisor harshly criticized a good worker publicly, leading to the worker becoming useless to the company and eventually leaving for a competitor where they performed well.
* **People generally think they are in the right**, and even when they recognize a mistake, they tend to justify it with reasons or excuses.
- Criticizing someone usually accomplishes little except **giving them something to argue against**.
- While it is possible to get someone to admit **they were wrong**—through panic, resignation, or reluctant acknowledgment—it almost always **creates resentment**.
- Criticism is the **least effective way to influence someone’s thoughts or actions**; it **may produce short-term compliance** but will likely leave **lasting bitterness**.
